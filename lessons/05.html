Fine-tuning and LoRA

Somebody already spent a month of GPU time teaching a model to read and write. You do not need to repeat that. You need to change a small part of its behaviour — and you can do that by training less than one percent of its numbers.

By the end you can

05 · 1You are almost never going to pretrain

In Lesson 04 you trained a GPT of about 15 million parameters from nothing, and you saw the price: hours of compute for a model that writes simple stories and little else. Pretraining is where general ability comes from, and it is expensive. SmolLM3-3B saw 11.2 trillion tokens, on 384 H100 GPUs for 24 days.

You will not buy that again. What you need is a model that already reads and writes English, made to answer in your format, about your domain, with your refusals. That is fine-tuning: cheap, minutes to hours on one GPU, on one to fifty thousand examples.

StageDataComputeWhat it changes
PretrainingTrillions of tokens384 H100 × 24 days for SmolLM3-3BGeneral ability: grammar, facts, reasoning
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)1k – 50k examplesMinutes to hours on one GPUFormat, tone, task behaviour
Preference alignment (DPO)Pairs of answersSimilar to SFTWhich of two acceptable answers it prefers

These are not three sizes of one thing. Pretraining teaches the model what language is. SFT teaches it what a good reply looks like. Preference training only sorts between replies that are already reasonable. If your model cannot do the task at all, no preference data will fix it.

Remember this

Fine-tuning cannot add knowledge the base model lacks. It reliably changes behaviour: format, style, when to stop, which tool to call. If you need new facts, use retrieval, not a fine-tune.

05 · 2The memory bill of full fine-tuning

Take a real model: SmolLM3-3B, 3.08 billion parameters. Loading it for inference in bf16 costs 6.2 GB. Most engineers stop the arithmetic there, then wonder why training crashes on a 16 GB machine.

Training does not store one number per parameter. It stores four. Lesson 01 gave the reason: AdamW keeps two running averages per parameter, and each parameter needs its own gradient. In bytes, with a float32 master copy of the weights:

bytes = N × ( 4 + 4 + 8 ) = 16 N N how many parameters the model has — all of them, embeddings included 4 the weight itself, float32 4 its gradient 8 AdamW's two running averages of that gradient

Lesson 04 wrote N too, but counted only non-embedding parameters, because that is what the compute law needs. Here it is every parameter, because every one needs an optimizer slot. A float32 number is 4 bytes, a bf16 number 2 — the table in Lesson 01.

16 bytes per parameter is the number to carry in your head. A 1-billion-parameter model costs 16 GB to train, and a 3.08-billion one 49.3 GB — before a single activation is stored.

Activations sit on top of all of this, and one of them is enormous. The logits tensor has shape (batch, sequence, vocabulary). With a batch of 8, a sequence of 2048 and a vocabulary of 128,256, that one tensor is about 4.2 GB in bf16.

The output layer is what makes it that big. It turns each position's vector into one score per vocabulary token. This is why TRL — Transformer Reinforcement Learning, Hugging Face's library of ready-made training loops — makes loss_type="chunked_nll" its default for SFT. nll is negative log-likelihood, the cross-entropy of Lesson 01. The setting sends only the graded positions through the output layer, and computes the loss in slices. TRL reports that this saves roughly 30% of the memory.

N = 3.08e9                      # SmolLM3-3B parameters
BYTES_PER_PARAM = 4 + 4 + 8     # fp32 weight + fp32 gradient + two AdamW averages

print(f"full fine-tune : {N * BYTES_PER_PARAM / 1e9:5.1f} GB")   #  49.3 GB

trainable = 30.2e6              # LoRA r=16 on all linear layers
print(f"LoRA  (bf16)   : {(2.0 * N + BYTES_PER_PARAM * trainable) / 1e9:5.1f} GB")   #   6.6 GB
print(f"QLoRA (4-bit)  : {(0.5 * N + BYTES_PER_PARAM * trainable) / 1e9:5.1f} GB")   #   2.0 GB

Look at the middle column. Gradients and optimizer state fall from 37 GB to half a gigabyte, because they exist only for parameters being trained. That collapse is the point of the next section.

05 · 3LoRA: two thin matrices beside a frozen one

LoRA means Low-Rank Adaptation, and it is one line of linear algebra. Freeze the original weight matrix. Beside it add a much cheaper matrix, built as the product of two thin ones, and train only those.

The maths, once
W = W₀ + (α / r) · BA W₀ the frozen original, d_out × d_in B d_out × r, initialised to all zeros A r × d_in, initialised at random r the rank you choose. Trainable numbers = r × (d_in + d_out)

A and B together are the adapter. It is saved as its own small file, about 60 MB for r = 16 on a 3 B model. The base weights beside it are never written to at all.

Because B starts at zero, the product BA starts at zero, so the adapted model at step 0 is exactly the model you downloaded. There is no accuracy cliff when you attach an adapter.

Count the numbers for one matrix. A query projection in SmolLM3-3B is 2048 × 2048, which is 4,194,304 parameters. The adapter at r = 16 is 16 × (2048 + 2048) = 65,536 — 1.6% of it. That 65,536 is also how many gradients you store, and how many AdamW averages you keep two of.

The frozen base still sits in memory, because the forward pass runs through it. What disappears is everything backward needs. That is why the gradient and optimizer bar collapses while the weight bar does not.

05 · 4Rank, alpha, and which modules to touch

Rank is the capacity dial: the width of the bottleneck between A and B. Every column of the update BA is built from the same r patterns, so r is how many independent patterns the update may use. Trainable parameters grow linearly with it.

Why can 16 patterns be enough for a 2048 × 2048 matrix? Full fine-tuning may change that matrix in any of 2048 patterns. But a narrow task needs no general rewrite. It needs one small correction, repeated: always answer in JSON, never apologise, prefer this label. That correction lives in a few patterns, so allowing only r of them costs little. Not nothing: at low rank LoRA reaches a slightly lower peak, and 05 · 8 shows what you get in exchange.

Now the real counts for SmolLM3-3B, LoRA on every linear layer. Each projection costs r × (d_in + d_out), so add up one layer:

one layer of SmolLM3-3B, per unit of ranktext

k_proj      2048 ->   512      2048 +   512  =   2,560   # 4 key/value groups
v_proj      2048 ->   512      2048 +   512  =   2,560
o_proj      2048 ->  2048      2048 +  2048  =   4,096
gate_proj   2048 -> 11008      2048 + 11008  =  13,056
up_proj     2048 -> 11008      2048 + 11008  =  13,056
down_proj  11008 ->  2048     11008 +  2048  =  13,056
                                     one layer =  52,480

36 layers x 52,480 = 1,889,280 trainable parameters per unit of rank

The project model is smaller. On SmolLM2-360M-Instruct that same r = 16 all-linear setting gives about 8.7 M trainable parameters, roughly 2.4% — a larger share, because there are fewer frozen parameters to hide them among. Expect a number near that from print_trainable_parameters().

Alpha is not a second capacity dial. It is a fixed scale on the adapter's output, applied as alpha / r. Dividing by r is what makes the best learning rate roughly independent of the rank: raise r and each pattern contributes less, so the update does not explode. With use_rslora=True the scale becomes alpha / sqrt(r), which behaves better at high rank.

So what value? Most recipes use alpha = 2r, making the scale 2. Keep that unless you have a reason; every block here follows it.

Which modules? In Lesson 03 you built the four attention projections as self.wq, self.wk, self.wv and self.wo. Hugging Face models call the same four q_proj, k_proj, v_proj and o_proj. Those are the names PEFT matches on. Older tutorials target only those. The 2025 Thinking Machines report LoRA Without Regret (linked under Go deeper) is direct: apply LoRA to all weight matrices, including the feed-forward layers (gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj). Attention-only at r = 16 on SmolLM3-3B is 7.7 M parameters — four times smaller, and raising the rank does not buy back the coverage you lost. PEFT now recommends target_modules="all-linear".

Two libraries do the work from here. PEFT — Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning — implements LoRA and attaches it to any transformers model. TRL wraps the Lesson 01 training loop as SFTTrainer and DPOTrainer. Both are in the toolkit install.

lora.pypython
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model

base = "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct"
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(base, dtype=torch.bfloat16)  # v5: dtype, not torch_dtype

cfg = LoraConfig(
    r=16,
    lora_alpha=32,
    lora_dropout=0.05,             # zero 5% of the adapter's activations each step
    target_modules="all-linear",   # every linear layer, not attention only
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
)

model = get_peft_model(model, cfg)
model.print_trainable_parameters()   # read the real number; never trust an estimate
Common trap

LoRA needs a much higher learning rate than full fine-tuning, about ten times higher. Copy a full fine-tuning rate into a LoRA run and nothing crashes. The loss barely moves, and you conclude LoRA does not work.

Several learning rates appear in this lesson. Here they are in one place, as starting points:

RunStart fromWhy
Full fine-tune, SFT1e-5Every weight moves. SFTConfig ships with 2e-5, the same ballpark
LoRA SFT1e-4Only the adapter moves — about ten times higher
Full fine-tune, DPO1e-6A small correction on a working model. DPOConfig's own default
LoRA + DPO5e-6Higher again, but preference training stays delicate

One rule sits under the whole table. Whatever a full fine-tune of the same stage wants, LoRA wants several times more: about ten on the SFT row, five on the more delicate DPO row. Both library defaults, 2e-5 and 1e-6, are full-fine-tuning values. Neither is a LoRA starting point. TRL's SmolLM3-3B reproduction used 1e-5 for its LoRA run against 1e-6 for the full fine-tune — the same shape, pitched lower for a larger model.

One more rule from that report: keep the effective batch size below about 32. LoRA tolerates large batches worse than full fine-tuning, and more rank does not repair it. Effective batch is per_device_train_batch_size × gradient_accumulation_steps, the gradient accumulation of Lesson 01.

05 · 5QLoRA, and what to do on a Mac

LoRA removed the gradients and the optimizer state. The frozen base weights are now the biggest bar left. QLoRA — quantized LoRA — removes most of that too: quantize the frozen base to 4 bits, and keep the adapters in bf16. The base is never updated, so the loss of precision in it is a one-time cost rather than something that compounds during training.

Lesson 07 explains 4-bit storage properly. Here you need only the shape of the idea: each weight becomes one of 16 levels, plus a scale shared by a small block. That is lossy, and a frozen base tolerates it because nothing writes to it. nf4 and double quantization below are Lesson 07 terms — skipping the detail is safe.

qlora.pypython
import torch
from transformers import BitsAndBytesConfig
from trl import SFTTrainer, SFTConfig
from peft import LoraConfig

bnb = BitsAndBytesConfig(
    load_in_4bit=True,
    bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",              # 4-bit NormalFloat
    bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.float16,   # a free T4 has no bf16; use torch.bfloat16 on Ampere or newer
    bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,         # quantize the scales too
)

trainer = SFTTrainer(                       # CUDA only — see the warning below
    model="HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM3-3B",
    args=SFTConfig(learning_rate=1e-4, max_length=2048, packing=True,
                   fp16=True),              # matches the compute dtype above; use bf16=True instead on Ampere
    train_dataset=ds,                       # ds: your dataset — built in sft.py below
    quantization_config=bnb,                # passed straight to the trainer since TRL v1.8.0
    peft_config=LoraConfig(r=256, lora_alpha=512, target_modules="all-linear"),
)
trainer.train()
Common trap

bitsandbytes is CUDA only. There is no backend for Metal Performance Shaders (MPS), PyTorch's GPU path on Apple Silicon, so the block above cannot run on your MacBook at all. Every QLoRA tutorial assumes an NVIDIA card and does not say so. On Apple Silicon you have two honest options. Run plain bf16 LoRA on a smaller base such as SmolLM2-360M-Instruct, which fits in unified memory. Or use a free Colab T4 for the QLoRA run. A T4 is a Turing card with no bf16, which is why the block above is float16 throughout. fp16=True is how you ask the trainer for it. It wraps each step in torch.amp.autocast and adds a GradScaler — the loss scaler of Lesson 04, which stops small fp16 gradients rounding to zero.

Apple Silicon, 16 GB

  • bf16 LoRA on a 0.3 B – 1 B base.
  • No bitsandbytes, no CUDA kernels.
  • Unified memory: no hard VRAM wall, so long sequences are easier.
  • Slower per step, but leave it running.

Free Colab T4, 16 GB of video memory (VRAM)

  • QLoRA works: 4-bit NF4 base, fp16 adapters.
  • fp16 plus a GradScaler everywhere — a Turing card has no bf16.
  • Idle-killed sessions, capped weekly quota.
  • Checkpoint often.

05 · 6Supervised fine-tuning in practice

One decision comes first. A base model has only been pretrained: it continues text and has no idea what a conversation is. An instruct model has been through SFT and ships a chat template on its tokenizer. Everything below depends on that template, so always start from an instruct model. The -Instruct suffix is how you tell.

The loss for SFT is the cross-entropy you know from Lesson 01: predict the next token, with the labels shifted one step left. Nothing new. What is new is the plumbing around it, and the plumbing is where fine-tunes fail.

Chat templates

A chat template is a small program stored on the tokenizer, written in Jinja — the same text-templating language web frameworks use to fill placeholders in a page. It turns a list of messages into one flat string with role markers, such as <|im_start|>user … <|im_end|>. The model has no concept of "messages"; it sees only that flat string of tokens.

Training and inference must use the same template. Otherwise the model is trained to stop after one token and asked to stop after a different one, so it answers correctly and then writes forever. A mismatched end-of-sequence token is the single most common cause of "my fine-tuned model never stops".

Masking the loss

If you hand TRL one flat text column, every position is graded, including the user's words. You do not want that. You are not training a model to write user questions.

The mask lives in the label tensor. Ignored positions get the value -100, which PyTorch's cross-entropy skips. TRL picks the behaviour from your dataset's shape:

Dataset columnsSettingWhat ends up in the loss
textdefaultEvery token
prompt + completioncompletion_only_loss=True (default for this shape)The completion only
messagesassistant_only_loss=TrueThe assistant turns only

assistant_only_loss=True needs the template to mark its assistant spans with {% generation %} blocks, so TRL knows where they are. TRL patches some known families automatically, and SmolLM3's template carries the markers. Neither SmolLM2-360M-Instruct nor Qwen3-0.6B is guaranteed to carry them in the copy you download. If they are missing, the setting quietly does nothing — and training on every token looks exactly like a successful run until you read the output.

There is a way out, and it is the row above in that table. If your template has no {% generation %} markers, rewrite the file with prompt and completion columns instead of messages. Apply the template yourself to everything up to the assistant's turn, and put the answer in completion. Then completion_only_loss=True masks the prompt for you. It masks by column boundary rather than by marker, so it works on any tokenizer.

So check it. Take one batch and print the tokens that survived the mask:

check_mask.pypython
tokenizer = trainer.processing_class          # build the SFTTrainer of sft.py first, then run this
batch     = next(iter(trainer.get_train_dataloader()))
labels    = batch["labels"][0]

print(tokenizer.decode(batch["input_ids"][0][labels != -100]))
print((labels != -100).sum().item(), "graded of", len(labels))

A correct run prints assistant answers and the tokens ending their turns, and nothing else — no system text, no user text. Expect several answers in a row rather than one, because packing=True below puts several whole examples in each sequence. A broken run prints the conversation entire, user turns included. Four lines, run once — the only thing between you and the model that never stops.

Now the file. Your training set is one JSON object per line — a .jsonl file. Each object holds a messages list, each message a role and a string. A system turn is allowed, and is graded like the user's: not at all. One line of a ticket classifier:

my_task.jsonl — one linejson
{"messages": [{"role": "system", "content": "You label support tickets."},
              {"role": "user", "content": "My card was charged twice."},
              {"role": "assistant", "content": "billing"}]}

The chat template turns that line into one flat string:

the same row, after apply_chat_templatetext
<|im_start|>system
You label support tickets.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
My card was charged twice.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
billing<|im_end|>

With assistant_only_loss=True, only the last two lines are graded: the word billing and the <|im_end|> that closes its turn. Everything above is labelled -100. How many tokens that is depends on how your tokenizer splits billing — a handful at most, and check_mask.py prints the exact count. That is the animation again, on a row you could write yourself.

sft.pypython
from datasets import load_dataset
from peft import LoraConfig
from trl import SFTTrainer, SFTConfig

ds = load_dataset("json", data_files="my_task.jsonl", split="train")   # rows of {"messages": [...]}

trainer = SFTTrainer(
    model="HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct",          # a model id string is enough now
    args=SFTConfig(
        output_dir="smol-360m-mytask",
        learning_rate=1e-4,               # ~10x a full fine-tune
        num_train_epochs=2,
        per_device_train_batch_size=4,
        gradient_accumulation_steps=4,    # effective batch 16 — stay under about 32
        max_length=1024,                  # TRL v1.10: max_length, not max_seq_length
        packing=True,                     # best-fit-decreasing: never splits an example
        assistant_only_loss=True,
        max_grad_norm=1.0,
    ),
    train_dataset=ds,
    peft_config=LoraConfig(r=16, lora_alpha=32, target_modules="all-linear"),
)
trainer.train()

One line deserves a note. Lesson 04 warned that naive wrapped packing cuts an assistant answer in half, teaching the model to stop mid-sentence. TRL's packing=True uses best-fit-decreasing packing: it fits whole examples into each sequence and never splits one. That is Lesson 04's promise, kept.

While it runs, TRL logs loss, entropy, mean_token_accuracy, num_tokens, learning_rate and grad_norm. The most readable is mean_token_accuracy: the fraction of graded positions where the model's top prediction was correct. A healthy SFT run moves it from about 0.45 to around 0.60 – 0.70. grad_norm is logged before clipping, so it is your early warning: a spike of ten to a hundred times means a bad batch is about to damage the model.

The data is the model

For SFT, quality beats quantity by a wide margin. A thousand examples you have actually read are worth more than a million scraped ones. Three habits, in order of importance:

  • Read fifty examples by hand. Every time. You will find broken formatting, truncated answers and duplicated prompts — in an hour, rather than after a training run.
  • Deduplicate, then decontaminate. Remove anything that also appears in your evaluation set, or you are measuring memorisation.
  • Generate with a bigger model, deliberately. Mainstream now: SmolLM3 used preference pairs generated by Qwen3. Check the licence on the teacher's outputs before you ship.

05 · 7Preference alignment: what DPO actually does

SFT teaches the model one correct answer per prompt. Preference training teaches it to prefer one answer over another, from pairs where a human or a bigger model marked one as better.

The classic route was reinforcement learning. There the model generates a whole answer and gets one score back — a single number for hundreds of tokens, not a target token at every position. One generate-and-score cycle is an episode, or rollout. That is very little feedback per unit of work, which is why it learns slowly. The recipe was a reward model plus PPO, Proximal Policy Optimization: four models in memory, and unstable. Almost nobody does that for small models now.

DPO — Direct Preference Optimization — removes the reward model entirely. It compares the current model with a frozen reference copy of itself, and pushes the chosen answer's log-probability up relative to the rejected one.

Δ(y) = log π(y|x) − log π_ref(y|x) L = − log σ( β · [ Δ(chosen) − Δ(rejected) ] ) π the model being trained π_ref a frozen copy of where it started log π(y|x) the log-probability of the whole answer y given the prompt x (the bar means "given"), summed over its tokens, as in Lesson 01 σ the sigmoid: it squashes any number into 0 to 1, so − log σ(z) is large when z is negative, small when z is large β how hard to stay near π_ref (default 0.1)

Nothing is sampled and no reward model is trained. It is one forward pass of each model over two answers, and a cross-entropy-shaped loss on the difference.

β · Δ(y) is called the implicit reward. DPO trains no reward model, but it behaves as if it had one, and that is the number TRL logs. Put small values through the whole thing once:

one preference pair, by handtext
log pi(chosen)     = -12.0      log pi_ref(chosen)   = -12.4
log pi(rejected)   =  -9.0      log pi_ref(rejected) =  -9.1

delta(chosen)     = -12.0 - (-12.4)  =  0.4
delta(rejected)   =  -9.0 - ( -9.1)  =  0.1
delta margin      =   0.4  -   0.1   =  0.3
beta x margin     =   0.1  x   0.3   =  0.03   # this is rewards/margins

loss = -log sigmoid(0.03) = 0.68               # -log sigmoid(0) = 0.693

0.693 is the loss of a coin flip; this pair scores 0.68, barely better. Two things follow. The rejected answer is still the more likely one in absolute terms, −9.0 against −12.0 — DPO moves only the gap. And the loss falls slowly at first, because the sigmoid is flat near zero. That is what "margins should grow" means.

dpo.pypython
from datasets import load_dataset
from trl import DPOTrainer, DPOConfig

trainer = DPOTrainer(
    model="Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B",
    args=DPOConfig(beta=0.1, learning_rate=1e-6, max_length=1024),
    train_dataset=load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized", split="train"),
)
trainer.train()
Common trap

Look at the learning rate. SFTConfig defaults to 2e-5, DPOConfig to 1e-6 — twenty times smaller. Those are the two full-fine-tuning rows of the 05 · 4 table. Copy an SFT rate into a DPO run and you destroy the model in a few hundred steps.

TRL logs three numbers, all of them the implicit reward in disguise. rewards/accuracies is the fraction of pairs where the chosen answer's implicit reward is higher; it should rise above 0.5 and settle around 0.7 – 0.9. rewards/margins is the average gap between the two — the 0.03 above — and it should grow. logps/chosen is log π(chosen) itself. If that falls too, DPO is suppressing everything instead of preferring the winner: lower the learning rate or raise β.

When is DPO worth it? After SFT works, and when you have real pairs — user thumbs, an A/B test, a bigger model judging two candidates. It is good at removing one persistent habit SFT keeps reproducing. For most on-device models a better SFT dataset helps more. Two neighbours are worth knowing. GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization — is the default when you have a verifiable reward, such as a unit test that passes. APO — Anchored Preference Optimization, the apo_zero and apo_down loss types — is what SmolLM3-3B used for its final alignment stage.

Worth knowing

This section is context, not path. Nothing later needs DPO, and the project below stops at SFT. It is here so you recognise the words on a model card.

05 · 8Measuring, forgetting, merging

Why benchmark numbers mislead for small models

Perplexity is only comparable between models that share a tokenizer and are measured on the same text. Across tokenizers, use bits-per-byte: divide the model's total loss on a passage by the number of bytes of that passage rather than by the number of tokens. Byte counts do not depend on the tokenizer, so two models with different vocabularies become comparable. Public benchmarks are worse, and under a billion parameters they are close to useless. Four mechanical reasons:

  • Multiple choice is scored by log-likelihood ranking, not by generation. The model never writes an answer; the harness scores each option and picks the highest. A small model can beat chance on MMLU — Massive Multitask Language Understanding, a 57-subject multiple-choice benchmark — while being unable to write one coherent sentence about the question. Under 1 B, MMLU sits near its 25% chance floor and is mostly noise.
  • Format sensitivity. Change A) to (A) and a small model's score moves several points. Large models are barely affected. Small ones are.
  • Contamination. Web corpora contain benchmark test sets, and quality-filtering does not remove them.
  • Aggregates hide what you care about. A 200 M model that summarises notes is not measured by a maths benchmark.

A large model as judge is the practical alternative, with three biases: position (it prefers whichever answer came first), length (longer answers) and self-preference (its own family). Mitigate all three: randomise the A/B order and average both directions, cap answer length, pick a judge from another family.

Remember this

Build a task-specific evaluation set of 50 – 200 examples by hand, before you train anything. For a device model it beats every public benchmark combined. It is the only number that tells you whether today's run beat yesterday's. When you do want the public suites, lighteval — Hugging Face's evaluation harness — runs them for you.

Catastrophic forgetting

That is the picture above: fine-tuning on a narrow dataset moves the weights toward that narrow distribution, and everything outside it gets worse.

The clearest reference is LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less (Biderman and others, TMLR 2024). Its finding is the trade promised in 05 · 4. At standard low ranks LoRA reaches a slightly lower peak on the target task than full fine-tuning. But it preserves ability outside that domain far better: the low-rank constraint is itself a regulariser.

Practical defences, cheapest first:

  1. Use LoRA rather than full fine-tuning.
  2. Train for fewer epochs — one to three passes. Overfitting and forgetting arrive together, and you can already spot overfitting from Lesson 01.
  3. Replay: mix 1 – 10% of general instruction data into your set.
  4. Lower the learning rate, and leave the embeddings frozen.
  5. Keep the adapter unmerged, so you can always go back to the base model.

And measure it: three general prompts, before and after.

Merging the adapter back

An attached adapter costs two extra matrix multiplies per targeted layer, on every token. Small, not free, and it makes exporting harder. Merging computes (alpha / r) · B · A once, adds it into W, and hands back an ordinary model with the base model's shape, file size and speed.

merge.pypython
import torch
from peft import PeftModel
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

base_id = "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct"
base = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(base_id, dtype=torch.bfloat16)
model = PeftModel.from_pretrained(base, "smol-360m-mytask")

merged = model.merge_and_unload()      # W becomes W + (alpha/r) B A, then the adapter is removed
merged.save_pretrained("smol-360m-mytask-merged")
AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(base_id).save_pretrained("smol-360m-mytask-merged")
Worth knowing

Merging is one-way, and costs two things: you can no longer swap adapters at runtime, and merging into a quantized base loses precision, because the sum is rounded back into 4 bits. So train with QLoRA, merge into the full-precision base, and quantize afterwards. Lesson 09 needs a single merged model — the export tools follow one graph of ordinary weights, not a base model plus a separate adapter file. Save both: the adapter for experiments, the merged model for shipping.

Project 05

LoRA fine-tune a small open model

Teach an open instruct model one narrow task you choose: classify support tickets, rewrite text into a fixed JSON shape, answer in one style. The task matters less than the discipline — build the evaluation set first, measure the base model, train, measure again, and measure what was lost.

  1. Pick an instruct base: HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct or Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B. Both are Apache-2.0 and ungated; gated repos block you at download time.
  2. Before anything else, write 100 evaluation examples by hand, and 3 general prompts that have nothing to do with your task. Record the base model's answers to all of them.
  3. Build 1,000 – 2,000 training examples as a .jsonl file, one object per line, in the shape shown in 05 · 6. Generate them with a larger model if you like, then read fifty yourself and fix what is broken.
  4. Attach LoRA: r=16, lora_alpha=32, target_modules="all-linear". Run print_trainable_parameters(): about 8.7 M on the 360 M base, the figure in 05 · 4.
  5. Build the SFTTrainer as in sft.py: learning rate 1e-4, 2 epochs, effective batch 16, assistant_only_loss=True. Do not call train() yet.
  6. Run check_mask.py on one batch of that trainer. If the user's words appear in the decoded output, assistant_only_loss is doing nothing, and training now would waste the run. Fix it first: rebuild the .jsonl with prompt and completion columns, which mask correctly without the template's generation markers. Re-run the check, then call trainer.train() and watch grad_norm and mean_token_accuracy.
  7. Evaluate on your 100 held-out examples, then rerun the 3 general prompts and compare with step 2. That difference is your forgetting.
  8. Merge with merge_and_unload() and save. Keep this file — Lesson 09 exports it.

Then break it on purpose. Each of these fails quietly, which is why you should see them once:

  • Set learning_rate=2e-5 — SFTConfig's own default, and a full-fine-tuning value. How far does the loss move over the same steps?
  • Turn assistant_only_loss off. Generate ten answers. How soon does the model write a user turn of its own?
  • Target the four attention projections only, keeping r=16. Compare trainable parameters and task score.
  • Train 10 epochs instead of 2, then rerun the 3 general prompts. That is catastrophic forgetting, with your own eyes.

Check yourself

You move a 3-billion-parameter model from full fine-tuning to LoRA with r = 16, keeping the frozen base in bf16. Which part of the memory bill falls the most?

Gradients and optimizer state exist only for trained parameters, so they fall from about 37 GB to half a gigabyte. The base weights only halve, and that is fp32 to bf16, not LoRA.

You attach LoRA with r = 32 to all linear layers of SmolLM3-3B, which costs 1,889,280 parameters per unit of rank. How many trainable parameters is that, and roughly how much memory do their gradients and AdamW state need?

1,889,280 × 32 = 60,456,960, about 60.5 M. Each trainable parameter still costs the 16 bytes of 05 · 2, so 60.5e6 × 16 ≈ 968 MB — call it 1 GB. Doubling the rank doubles both numbers.

Training at r = 256 costs far more memory than r = 16. After merging, what happens to the cost of generating one token?

Merging computes (alpha / r) · B A once, and that product is d_out × d_in whatever r was. It is added into W and vanishes. Rank is a training-time bill, never an inference-time one.

Your fine-tuned model gives a good answer, then invents a new user question and keeps writing until it hits the token limit. What do you check first?

A model that will not stop was either trained with a different end-of-sequence token than generation stops on, or trained on every token, so writing the user's turn is what it learned. Both live in the template, not the adapter.

Key words from this lesson
Adapter
The two thin matrices LoRA trains, saved as their own file beside the unchanged base model.
Rank written r
The width of the bottleneck between them, and so how much the adapter can change.
Alpha written lora_alpha
A fixed scale on the adapter's output, alpha divided by rank. Not extra capacity. Use 2r.
QLoRA
Quantized LoRA: the frozen base is stored in 4 bits, so the last big bar shrinks too.
Chat template
A small program on the tokenizer that turns a list of messages into one flat string of tokens.
Ignore index the value -100
The label value that tells the loss to skip a position, so it produces no gradient.
SFT supervised fine-tuning
Training on example conversations, to fix format and behaviour.
DPO direct preference optimization
Training on pairs of answers marked better and worse, with no reward model.
Catastrophic forgetting
The model gets better at your narrow task and quietly worse at everything else.
Merging
Adding the adapter's product into the base weights, leaving one ordinary model.
