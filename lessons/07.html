07

Quantization: fewer bits

A model is a pile of numbers. Quantization puts those numbers in a smaller container. The architecture does not change, no training is repeated, and the file gets four to eight times smaller. This lesson is the arithmetic behind that, and the honest way to check what it cost.

Part III · Make it small ≈ 105 min reading Project · FP32 to INT4, measured honestly Needs · Lessons 04, 05

By the end you can

  • Compute a model's real size in any format, metadata included.
  • Write the quantize / dequantize round trip from memory, name every symbol, bound its error.
  • Cost per-tensor, per-channel and per-group scales.
  • Explain why activations are harder to quantize than weights.
  • Read a filename such as Q4_K_M and know what is inside it.
  • Measure the damage with a perplexity delta and a task score, not a feeling.

07 · 1The container, not the model

Lesson 01 gave you the arithmetic: a 1-billion-parameter model in FP32 is 4 GB of weights. Lesson 06 made a model smaller by removing capacity, a small student learning from a large teacher. Quantization removes no capacity at all. Every weight survives, stored with fewer bits.

Why this matters on a phone is not arithmetic speed. Generating one token reads every weight once and does very little maths per weight. So the limit is how many bytes per second the memory can deliver. That is what memory-bound means, and it makes fewer bytes per weight your biggest lever. How much of that saving becomes a faster token is a separate question, measured in Lesson 08.

So quantization is a storage decision, not an architecture decision. Everything difficult about it comes from one fact: a small integer cannot represent every real number, so it takes the nearest it can and throws the difference away.

07 · 2The formats, and what each one costs

Every download page throws format names at you: BF16, FP8, INT4, NF4. The prefixes are the easy part. FP is floating point, INT integer, BF bfloat — the "brain float" from Google Brain — and NF NormalFloat. The number after is the total bit width.

A floating point number is three fields: a sign bit, exponent bits, mantissa bits. The exponent decides the range, how large and small a number can be. The mantissa decides the precision, how many values sit between two powers of two. An integer format has neither. It is an evenly spaced counter, meaningless until you attach a scale.

Read E4M3 as the field split: E is exponent bits, M mantissa bits. So FP8 E4M3 is 1, 4 and 3 — exactly the third column — and FP4 E2M1 is 1, 2 and 1.

The contrast to hold on to: integer levels are evenly spaced, floating point levels are not. FP4 is fine near zero, coarse far from it — a reasonable match for weight distributions, which are bell-shaped and centred on zero. NF4 pushes the idea further, placing its 16 levels at the quantiles of a normal distribution.

Now the question that decides everything: what does each format cost in megabytes? Take the 360-million-parameter model you fine-tuned in Lesson 05, counting weights only. One megabyte is 106 bytes here.

Common trap

"Four-bit" is never four bits. Every group carries a scale, usually a zero point too. Group 128 with an FP16 scale and zero point is 32 extra bits per 128 weights: 4.25 bits per weight. At group 32 it is 5.0. Divide a file size by the parameter count, get 4.5 instead of 4.0, and the file is not lying. You forgot the metadata.

That 191 MB is not an abstract target. It is the brain the offline voice assistant of Lesson 13 carries, on a model budget of about 200 MB.

An anchor at the other end. OpenAI's gpt-oss models ship natively in MXFP4 — a 4.25-bit format explained in section 7 — for their mixture-of-experts weights. Such a model replaces each block's feed-forward network, the multi-layer perceptron or MLP, with many parallel copies called experts. A small network, the router, sends each token to two or three of them. Two facts matter here: the expert weights are over 90% of the parameters, and the router is tiny and must stay accurate. At 4.25 bits the 117-billion-parameter gpt-oss-120b fits on one 80 GB GPU, against roughly 240 GB at BF16.

07 · 3Scale and zero point

An INT8 value of 73 is meaningless alone. Turning integers back into real numbers needs a scale, the size of one step, and optionally a zero point, the integer mapping to exactly 0.0.

The maths, once

The legend first. w is one real weight. min and max are the smallest and largest real value in the group being quantized. qmin and qmax are the smallest and largest integer the format holds: 0 and 15 for unsigned INT4, −8 and 7 for signed INT4, −128 and 127 for signed INT8. clamp(v, a, b) keeps v inside a…b. q is the integer you store; ŵ, said "w-hat", is what you get back.

s = (max − min) / (qmax − qmin) the scale z = qmin − round(min / s) the zero point q = clamp(round(w/s) + z, qmin, qmax) quantize ŵ = s × (q − z) dequantize

This is affine, or asymmetric, quantization. It fits the integer range to whatever real range your numbers occupy. The zero point slides the grid onto the data. When the group's range contains zero — that is, when min ≤ 0 ≤ max — it also puts real 0.0 exactly on the grid. That matters, because padding and ReLU produce a great many exact zeros.

Work it once, on four numbers you can check on a calculator. Take one group of positive values: [0.6, 1.1, 1.7, 2.1]. Store them in unsigned INT4, qmin = 0 and qmax = 15.

s = (2.1 − 0.6) / (15 − 0) = 0.1 z = 0 − round(0.6 / 0.1) = −6 q = 0, 5, 11, 15 ŵ = 0.1 × (q + 6) = 0.6, 1.1, 1.7, 2.1

Every value came back exactly, because the grid was fitted to the data. But watch the condition above. This group's minimum is 0.6, so 0.0 is not on this grid. Push it through: q = clamp(round(0/0.1) − 6, 0, 15) = 0, and that reads back as 0.6. Exact zeros survive only when the group's range reaches down to zero.

Force z = 0 instead and you get symmetric quantization, where the grid is centred on zero and you store one number per group:

s = max|w| / qmax qmax = 127 for INT8, 7 for signed INT4 q = clamp(round(w/s), −qmax, qmax) ŵ = s × q

Run the same four numbers through that. The scale becomes 2.1 / 7 = 0.3, three times coarser, because the seven negative levels go to numbers that never occur. You get back 0.6, 1.2, 1.8, 2.1 — two of the four have moved. That is what a zero point buys: it slides the grid onto the data.

Reconcile that with the format table, which says INT4 spans −8 to 7. Symmetric INT4 uses −7 to 7 and never emits −8, because zero must sit exactly in the middle. The same reasoning gives symmetric INT8 the range −127 to 127. That dropped level causes "my reimplementation is slightly wrong and I cannot find why". Write a kernel with 128 and compare it against a library that used 127. The outputs differ by a fraction of a percent, and no error is raised.

Symmetric is cheaper at inference time — the −z cross terms disappear from the matrix multiply — and almost all weight quantization uses it. Asymmetric is better when the distribution is one-sided, as activations after ReLU or GELU are.

The round trip loses something, and the loss is bounded. The stored value is on the grid; the true value is at most half a step away:

|ŵ − w| ≤ s / 2

That is the worst case; the typical case is more useful. Treat the rounding error as spread evenly over that interval and its mean squared error — the average of the squared error — is s² / 12. With s = 0.25 that is 0.0625 / 12 = 0.0052, so a typical error is √0.0052 ≈ 0.072, neatly inside the 0.00 to 0.12 that roundtrip.py prints below. Halve the scale to 0.125 and it falls to 0.0013, four times smaller. Halving the scale quarters the squared error. And the scale is set by the largest magnitude in the group — the next two sections follow from that.

roundtrip.pypython
import torch

w = torch.tensor([-0.42, 0.13, 1.75, -0.08, 0.55, -1.20, 0.31, 0.02])

qmax = 7                                  # signed INT4, symmetric
s = w.abs().max() / qmax                  # 1.75 / 7 = 0.25
q = torch.clamp(torch.round(w / s), -qmax, qmax).to(torch.int8)
w_hat = q.to(w.dtype) * s                 # dequantize

print(q)              # the codes:  -2, 1, 7, 0, 2, -5, 1, 0
print(w_hat)          # what you get back: -0.50, 0.25, 1.75, 0.00, 0.50, -1.25, 0.25, 0.00
print((w_hat - w).abs().max().item())     # about 0.12, and s/2 = 0.125

07 · 4Granularity: how many scales do you keep?

One scale for a whole 4096 × 4096 matrix means 16.7 million weights sharing a single step size, chosen by whichever single weight happens to be largest. That is the cheapest option and the worst. The alternatives cost metadata, and the metadata is small:

GranularityScales for a 4096 × 4096 layerExtra bits / weightRobustness
Per-tensor1≈ 0Worst. One outlier anywhere sets the scale.
Per-channel (per row)4 0960.004Good for weights. The standard for INT8.
Per-group, group = 128131 0720.25The standard for INT4.
Per-group, group = 32524 2881.0Best accuracy, biggest overhead.

Check the second row yourself; the project asks for it. One FP16 scale per row is 4 096 × 16 = 65 536 bits over 16.8 million weights: 0.004 extra bits per weight. Group 128 is larger only because there are far more groups — 131 072 × 32 bits, a scale and a zero point, ÷ 16.8 million = 0.25.

For activations the natural axis differs. An activation tensor is [batch, sequence, features], and the useful choice is one scale per token, computed on the fly. One rule decides all of it:

Remember this

Put your scales on an axis the matrix multiply does not sum over. Two terms of a dot product show why: s·q₁·x₁ + s·q₂·x₂ = s·(q₁x₁ + q₂x₂). You can lift s out only because it is the same in both terms. Give them different scales and there is nothing to lift out, so the integer matrix multiply stops being an integer matrix multiply.

Group-wise quantization looks like a violation of that rule. It is not. Group-128 scales do split the reduction axis, and the kernel copes by accumulating one integer partial sum per group, rescaling each, then adding them. The rule holds inside each group; the kernel just pays for more rescales.

That is also the practical warning about group size. A group of 32 is more accurate than 128, but may land on a slower code path, because the kernel reloads a scale four times more often. Accuracy and speed do not always move together, so measure both — Lesson 08 teaches how.

07 · 5Outliers, and why activations are harder

Weights are well behaved: roughly bell-shaped, centred on zero, no extreme values. Activations inside a large language model are not. Lesson 06 met these outliers already — they are why Wanda scores a weight by |w| × ‖x‖ rather than |w| alone. Here is what one does. Take the eight-weight group above and change one value from 1.75 to 17.50.

The scale went from 0.25 to 2.50, and seven of the eight weights now round to exactly zero. One outlier destroyed the group. That picture justifies group-wise quantization and every method named below.

Now the part that makes activation quantization genuinely hard. The LLM.int8() work measured transformers as they grow. Above about 6.7 billion parameters the behaviour changes suddenly and stays changed: the same outlier appears in all layers, in the same feature dimension, for about 75% of sequence positions. Roughly 150 000 outlier values per sequence, in only 6 feature dimensions, about 20 times larger than every other channel. Set that 0.1% of features to zero and validation perplexity degrades by 600% or more.

So the outliers are enormous, rare and essential. Three responses exist, and you meet all three in tool names:

  1. Mixed-precision decomposition (LLM.int8()): send the ~0.1% outlier columns through FP16, the rest through INT8. Correct, but it breaks the single clean matmul, so it is slow.
  2. Migrate the difficulty (SmoothQuant): divide the activations by a per-channel factor and multiply the weights by the same factor. Two numbers show the output does not change. An activation of 8 times a weight of 0.1 is 0.8. Divide the activation by 4 and multiply the weight by 4: 2 × 0.4 is still 0.8. The outlier shrank fourfold and nothing else moved. Weights tolerate being larger; activations could not. The knob is the migration strength, from 0 to 1: the factor is the channel's largest activation raised to that power, divided by its largest weight raised to one minus it. At 0 the activations are untouched, at 1 all the difficulty lands on the weights, and 0.5 splits it.
  3. Protect the weight channels that matter most (AWQ, activation-aware weight quantization): about 1% of weight channels matter, and you find them by activation magnitude, not weight magnitude. Scale those channels up before quantizing, which cuts their relative error. AWQ needs calibration data: a few hundred sample sequences pushed through the model, to record the ranges its activations reach. There is no backward pass, so it cannot overfit that data.

That splits the field in two. Decode the shorthand once: W is weight bits, A is activation bits. W4A16 is 4-bit weights multiplied in 16-bit arithmetic; W8A8 is both sides in 8 bits.

Weight-only — W4A16, W8A16

  • INT4 weights, dequantized to BF16 inside the kernel, matmul in BF16.
  • Wins on memory bandwidth, the limit on one-user generation.
  • GPTQ — one column at a time, adjusting the columns not yet done to compensate the error already made. Needs calibration data.
  • AWQ — protects the 1% of channels the activations say matter.
  • HQQ — no calibration data; fits the scale with a loss that refuses to chase outliers.
  • bitsandbytes — the NF4 and INT8 backend from Lesson 05.
  • GGUF k-quants (section 7) and MLX 4-bit, Apple Silicon's group-wise format.

Weight and activation — W8A8, W4A4

  • Both operands low precision, so the matmul runs on integer or FP4 units.
  • Wins on compute, the limit on prefill and large-batch serving.
  • Needs outlier handling, often specific hardware.
  • SmoothQuant, FP8, MXFP4, NVFP4.

On your own device, serving one user, weight-only is almost always right: you are memory-bound, not compute-bound.

07 · 6PTQ, QAT, and the straight-through estimator

So far you quantize a finished model and accept what the rounding costs. Usually that is fine. When it is not, one move remains: let the model keep training while it can feel the rounding. Two regimes, and the second costs far more.

PTQ — post-training quantization — takes a trained model, computes the scales, writes out integers. Minutes to hours, no gradients. Almost all on-device deployment uses it, and so will your project.

Calibration — the sample-sequence pass from section 5 — is needed when activation scales are baked into the file rather than computed at runtime. Two flavours, and the difference matters:

  • Dynamic: the scale is computed at runtime, per token, from the tensor in front of you. No calibration, robust, tiny cost. The default for decoding.
  • Static: the scale is fixed at calibration time. Faster, no extra reduction pass, but fragile — activations outside the calibrated range get clipped. Common on neural processing units (NPUs) and in vision.

Calibration data leaks into behaviour, and this surprises people. Scales computed on English web text measurably underperform on code — a distribution shift baked into the file, not a rounding detail. If your model answers support tickets, calibrate on support tickets.

QAT — quantization-aware training — inserts fake quantize nodes into the forward pass during a short fine-tune. Fake quantize means quantize, then immediately dequantize, staying in floating point. The model feels the rounding error while it can still move in response.

There is one obstacle. round() has a derivative of exactly zero almost everywhere, and none at all at the steps. Differentiate the fake-quantize node honestly and every gradient is zero, so nothing trains.

The straight-through estimator is the fix, and it is a deliberate lie about the backward pass:

The maths, once
forward: ŵ = s × clamp(round(w/s), qmin, qmax) backward: ∂L/∂w := ∂L/∂ŵ × 1[ qmin ≤ w/s ≤ qmax ]

The gradient is passed through unchanged inside the clipping range and set to zero outside it. In PyTorch you write this in one line, using the fact that detach() removes a term from the graph without changing its value:

ste.pypython
def fake_quantize(w, s, qmax=7):
    q = torch.clamp(torch.round(w / s), -qmax, qmax)
    w_q = q * s                                   # the rounded value
    return w + (w_q - w).detach()                 # value of w_q, gradient of w

The forward value is w_q, because w + (w_q − w) = w_q. The backward pass sees only w, because the bracket was detached. One line, and the staircase is differentiable enough.

QAT is real but not magic. torchao reports how much of the BF16-to-INT4 gap its weight-only QAT recovers: for Llama3.2-3B, 38.4% on held-out WikiText and 40.1% on a multi-step reasoning benchmark; for Gemma3, 33.7% and 66.9%. Expect a third to two thirds of the loss back, for the price of a training run.

PTQ — do this first

  • Minutes. No gradients, no dataset for weight-only.
  • 8-bit weight-only: assume no loss.
  • 4-bit with a good method: usually enough.
  • Good enough? Stop. It usually is.

QAT — when PTQ is not enough

  • A full fine-tuning run, with data and a loss curve.
  • Recovers a third to two thirds of what PTQ lost.
  • Worth it below 4 bits, or when one task broke.
  • Combines with LoRA (low-rank adaptation), so few parameters train.
quantize.pypython
import torch
from torchao.quantization import quantize_, Int8WeightOnlyConfig, Int4WeightOnlyConfig
from torchao.quantization.granularity import PerGroup

# 8-bit weight-only. The safe first step: expect no measurable loss.
quantize_(model, Int8WeightOnlyConfig(granularity=PerGroup(128)))

# 4-bit weight-only, group 128 -> 4.25 bits per weight. quantize_ works in place.
quantize_(model, Int4WeightOnlyConfig(group_size=128))

# CPU and mobile path: int8 activations, int4 weights, group 32.
from torchao.quantization import Int8DynamicActivationIntxWeightConfig
quantize_(model, Int8DynamicActivationIntxWeightConfig(
    weight_dtype=torch.int4, weight_granularity=PerGroup(32)))

Note the API shape: quantize_(model, ConfigObject), in place, with config dataclasses. Older tutorials pass strings or call int4_weight_only(); the current documentation does not. These libraries are renamed and moved often — AutoGPTQ became GPTQModel, HQQ changed repositories. Check every import path against the current docs.

qat.pypython
from torchao.quantization import quantize_, Int4WeightOnlyConfig
from torchao.quantization.qat import QATConfig

base_config = Int4WeightOnlyConfig(group_size=32)

quantize_(model, QATConfig(base_config, step="prepare"))   # nn.Linear -> FakeQuantizedLinear
train_loop(model)                                          # your normal loop from Lesson 01
quantize_(model, QATConfig(base_config, step="convert"))   # -> real quantized Linear

07 · 7Reading a quantization name

Download a model to run locally and you meet filenames such as model-Q4_K_M.gguf. The GPT-Generated Unified Format (GGUF) is llama.cpp's single-file format; the suffix says exactly how the weights were stored. Take it apart:

  • Q — quantized to integers.
  • 4 — the nominal bit width of the weights.
  • K — a k-quant: a super-block scheme where the per-sub-block scales and minimums are themselves quantized, rather than stored as FP16. The same trick as bnb_4bit_use_double_quant, which you switched on in Lesson 05.
  • M — the size variant, S / M / L. Larger variants keep some tensors at higher precision, usually the attention value and feed-forward down projections, which is why Q4_K_M is bigger than Q4_K_S at the same nominal width.

Do the arithmetic inside Q4_K once; it makes metadata concrete. A super-block holds 256 weights as 8 sub-blocks of 32. It stores a 2-byte FP16 super-scale and a 2-byte minimum. Then one scale and one minimum per sub-block, 6 bits each: 8 × 2 × 6 = 96 bits = 12 bytes. Then the weights: 256 × 4 bits = 128 bytes. Add them. 2 + 2 + 12 + 128 = 144 bytes for 256 weights, and 144 × 8 ÷ 256 = 4.50 bits per weight.

That is plain Q4_K. The _M variant's protected tensors lift the whole-file average to about 4.89 bits per weight. That is the number Lessons 08 and 09 use, and why a Q4_K_M file is larger than 4.50 predicts.

The llama.cpp repo publishes the whole ladder on LLaMA-7B as perplexity added over FP16: Q2_K +0.87, labelled extreme loss; Q3_K_M +0.24; Q4_K_M +0.054, recommended; Q6_K +0.0044; Q8_0 +0.0004, wasteful. Q4_K_M is the default for a reason.

Two more naming families. I-quants — IQ2_XS, IQ3_S, IQ4_XS — are codebook based rather than uniform, reach lower effective bits per weight than the same k-quant, and need an imatrix. An importance matrix comes from running calibration text through the model and recording the energy — the sum of squares — each activation column carries. The quantizer weights its objective by that, changing which rounding is chosen, not the bit width.

Then two microscaling standards, because hardware is moving this way. MXFP4 uses E2M1 elements in blocks of 32 with one shared 8-bit power-of-two scale: 4.25 bits per weight. NVFP4 uses blocks of 16 with an FP8 E4M3 scale plus a per-tensor FP32 scale: 4.5 bits, finer blocks and a scale with a mantissa at twice the overhead. NVIDIA reports a 4-bit DeepSeek-R1 within 1% of its 8-bit scores on two hard reasoning benchmarks — but only on hardware running the format natively.

Otherwise you get the memory saving and no speed-up at all. Without native 4-bit units the kernel dequantizes to BF16 and multiplies in BF16: you keep the memory win, the main one when serving a single user, and gain nothing in arithmetic. Quantized does not mean faster. Lesson 08 shows how to time it, so you know instead of assuming.

07 · 8Measuring the damage

This is the part most tutorials skip. Quantization fails silently: the model loads, writes fluent text, and is a little worse. You need numbers.

First, perplexity. From Lesson 04, perplexity is the exponential of the cross-entropy loss. Run both models over the same held-out text with the same tokenizer and compare. Cheap, sensitive to broad damage, and the first thing to check.

measure.pypython
import math, torch

@torch.no_grad()
def perplexity(model, ids, window=1024):
    model.eval()
    total, count = 0.0, 0
    for i in range(0, ids.size(1) - window, window):
        chunk = ids[:, i:i + window]
        out = model(chunk, labels=chunk)        # HF models return a mean CE loss
        total += out.loss.item() * (window - 1)
        count += window - 1
    return math.exp(total / count)

ppl_fp = perplexity(base_model, ids)
ppl_q4 = perplexity(quant_model, ids)
print(f"{ppl_fp:.3f} -> {ppl_q4:.3f}   delta {ppl_q4 - ppl_fp:+.3f}")

What those columns measure. WikiText-2 is held-out encyclopedia text, used here for perplexity (PPL). GSM8K is grade-school word problems needing several arithmetic steps in a row. MMLU, massive multitask language understanding, is multiple-choice knowledge questions. HellaSwag is common-sense sentence completion. One needs a chain of reasoning, the other a stored fact.

Read the bottom row carefully. At Q3_K_S, GSM8K falls 9.3 points while HellaSwag moves 0.6. Reasoning degrades first; recall and common sense last. Look only at perplexity and you see 7.32 become 8.96, with no idea which ability you spent.

Now the Q5_0 row: GSM8K is 79.1 against FP16's 77.6. The quantized model scored higher. That is benchmark noise, and it is the most useful row here, because it sizes the measurement error you are working inside.

Third, read the outputs. Fix ten prompts, set temperature to zero, generate before and after. Numbers cannot see a model that repeats itself, loses a language or drops its chat format. Your eyes can.

How much loss is acceptable
  • 8-bit weight-only: free. Assume no loss.
  • 4-bit weight-only, group 128, good method: 1–2% relative loss. Fine for nearly anything you ship.
  • 3-bit: visible. Maths and multi-step reasoning suffer first.
  • 2-bit: only with i-quants and an imatrix, on large models. At equal file size a 2-bit large model usually beats a 4-bit small one.
Two ways to fool yourself

Never compare your quantized score to a number from a paper. Prompt template, temperature and harness version move scores more than 4-bit quantization does, so run your own FP16 baseline in your own harness. And the key-value (KV) cache is a separate decision: at long context it, not the weights, dominates memory. A 4-bit model with an FP16 cache leaves most of your memory problem unsolved.

One last check before you blame the bit width: not every tensor gets quantized. Embeddings, the output head, the normalisation layers and the mixture-of-experts router are usually kept at higher precision — gpt-oss excludes all four from its 4-bit conversion. They are a small share of the parameters and a large share of the damage. If your model collapsed, check whether your tool converted something it should have left alone.

Check yourself

You store a model in INT4 at group size 64, keeping one FP16 scale and one INT8 zero point per group. What is the true bits per weight?

Metadata is 16 + 8 = 24 bits per group of 64 weights, so 24 ÷ 64 = 0.375 extra bits per weight, and 4 + 0.375 = 4.375. This configuration appears nowhere in the lesson: you can now cost any of them.

Your INT4 model has almost the same perplexity as the BF16 original, but now fails multi-step arithmetic it used to answer. Most likely explanation?

Perplexity averages over every token, most of them easy. At Q3_K_S GSM8K fell 9.3 points while HellaSwag moved 0.6. Always evaluate one reasoning task.

You write fake quantize as w_q = q * s and return w_q directly, without detach(). You run a few training steps. What do you see?

The chain rule multiplies everything upstream by the derivative of round(), which is 0 between the steps. So every gradient reaching w is zero and the optimizer has nothing to apply. The straight-through estimator replaces that derivative with 1 inside the clipping range: w + (w_q - w).detach().

Key words from this lesson
Scale often written s
The size of one step on the integer grid. It turns a stored integer back into a real value.
Zero point also: offset
The integer that maps to exactly 0.0. Symmetric quantization forces it to 0 and stores nothing.
Symmetric quantization
The grid is centred on zero; the scale comes from the largest magnitude in the group.
Group size
How many weights share one scale. Smaller groups cost more metadata, lose less accuracy.
Outlier channel
A feature dimension whose activations dwarf every other dimension, in every layer.
Weight-only quantization written W4A16
Weights stored small, expanded in the kernel; arithmetic still in 16 bits. W is weight bits, A is activation bits.
Mixture of experts MoE
Many parallel copies of a block's feed-forward network, plus a router sending each token to two or three.
Calibration data
A few hundred sample sequences pushed through the model, recording the activation ranges reached.
Straight-through estimator STE
Round in the forward pass; pretend in the backward pass that the rounding was the identity.
imatrix importance matrix
A record of which columns matter most, used to choose better rounding at the same bit width.
Perplexity delta
Quantized perplexity minus original, on the same text.
