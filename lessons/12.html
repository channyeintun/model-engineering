12 · 1The reverse journey

In Lesson 11 you cut audio into short windows, turned each window into a mel spectrogram column, and asked a model which letters were in it. Speech synthesis, usually written TTS for text to speech, runs the same road in the other direction. The direction matters enormously.

Recognition compresses. One second of audio, 16,000 numbers, becomes maybe three words. Synthesis expands. Three words become one second of audio. The whole design of a TTS system is an answer to two questions: how long should each sound last, and what does the waveform look like inside that span.

One practical difference before the shapes. Recognition in Lesson 11 ran at 16 kHz, because that is enough to tell words apart. Synthesis here runs at 24 kHz, because a voice sampled only 16,000 times a second sounds dull and muffled. A resample between the two is a real pipeline step, and the assistant in Lesson 13 has to do it.

Here is one real sentence, 5.3 seconds long, at a sample rate of 24 kHz, as it moves through the pipeline. Read the last column.

StageTensor shapeNumbers
Characters[1, 78]78
Phoneme ids[1, 62]62
Encoder hidden states[1, 62, 192]11,904
After duration expansion[1, 500, 192]96,000
Mel spectrogram[1, 80, 500]40,000
Waveform, 24 kHz[1, 128000]128,000

From 62 symbols to 128,000 samples is an expansion of roughly 2000 times. That single ratio explains most of what follows, including why the last stage usually costs more compute than everything before it put together.

Remember this

A TTS system is a length-expansion machine with a text front end bolted on the left. The front end is rules and lookup tables. The expansion is the neural network. People spend all their time on the network and get bitten by the front end.

12 · 2Text normalisation: the part everyone forgets

A perfect acoustic model and a perfect vocoder will pronounce the wrong string perfectly. That is why this unglamorous stage comes first. It is where most of the bugs your users will actually report.

Text normalisation converts written text into the words a voice must actually pronounce. In a small system it is almost never a neural network. It is regular expressions, a locale setting, and a long list of special cases.

The hard cases repeat in every language, and none of them are solved by a bigger model:

Common trap

When a small TTS "sounds broken", the first suspect is normalisation, not the model. Print the normalised string before you print anything else. KittenTTS ships explicit preprocessing for numbers, currencies and units for exactly this reason — the model is 15 million parameters and it will not guess your intent.

12 · 3Letters to sounds

A model that reads letters has to learn English spelling and English sound from the same few hundred hours of audio. A model that reads sound symbols only has to learn sound. That single move is why a 15-million-parameter voice is possible at all, and it is why every small system has this stage.

A grapheme is a written letter. A phoneme is a unit of sound that changes meaning: swap one and you get a different word. English has about 44 phonemes but only 26 letters, and the mapping between them is famously irregular.

Grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, usually written G2P, is the stage that turns the normalised string into phonemes. The standard symbol set is the international phonetic alphabet (IPA) — /ˈmɒdəl/. Older American systems use an alternative called ARPAbet, written in plain ASCII characters, where the same word is M AA1 D AH0 L.

Lexicon lookup

  • A dictionary of word to pronunciation. CMUdict holds about 134,000 English words.
  • Exact, auditable, and easy to patch for one bad word.
  • Says nothing about a word it has never seen — names, brands, new words.

Learned or rule-based fallback

  • Guesses a pronunciation from the letters alone.
  • eSpeak NG is the workhorse here: a small C program whose front end many neural systems reuse.
  • Wrong sometimes, but never silent. Real systems use a lexicon first and a fallback second.

Piper embeds eSpeak NG directly for phonemisation. Kokoro uses a library called misaki for G2P, with eSpeak NG as the fallback for American and British English. This is worth knowing before you ship: eSpeak NG is licensed under the GPL, which is exactly why Piper moved from an MIT licence to GPL-3.0.

Remember this

About 60 phoneme symbols cover English. That small, regular alphabet is the single biggest reason a model of 15 to 82 million parameters can sound acceptable at all. The price is a front end you have to ship, license and debug — and it is worth every byte.

12 · 4The acoustic model: duration is rhythm

Phonemes carry no time. The word "model" is five symbols whether you say it slowly or quickly, and nothing in that list says the vowel lasts three times as long as the final consonant. Something has to decide the timing. Get it wrong and the voice sounds like a robot reading a shopping list. The acoustic model takes phonemes and produces a mel spectrogram with the right rhythm inside it.

The interface contract

The spectrogram here is the same kind of recipe you used for recognition in Lesson 11, but not the same numbers. Recognition ran at 16 kHz with n_fft=400, hop_length=160 and power=2.0, which is 100 columns per second. Synthesis runs at 24 kHz with n_fft=1024, hop_length=256 and power=1.0, which is 93.75 columns per second. Only n_mels=80 is shared. Reuse the Lesson 11 recipe here and you get audio at the wrong speed.

That is the point of the subsection. Whichever recipe you pick, it must match the vocoder's expectation exactly. Get the hop length wrong and the vocoder still runs. It simply produces audio at the wrong speed, with no error message anywhere. The project reproduces that family of silent failure on purpose, with a mislabelled sample rate.

The maths, once
frames per second = sample rate / hop length = 24000 / 256 = 93.75 mel(f) = 2595 · log10(1 + f / 700)

f is a frequency in hertz, and mel(f) is where that frequency sits on the axis a human ear uses. With n_fft = 1024, hop_length = 256 and n_mels = 80, one frame covers about 10.7 ms, and a 5.3-second sentence is 500 frames.

Put two numbers into the mel curve and the compression stops being an assertion. 1000 Hz gives 2595 · log10(1 + 1.43) = 1000 mel. 4000 Hz gives 2595 · log10(1 + 5.71) = about 2146 mel. Four times the frequency, only about twice the distance — because that is how human hearing works.

mel.pypython
import soundfile as sf, torch, torchaudio

wave, sr = sf.read("clip.wav", dtype="float32")     # must already be 24 kHz
wave = torch.from_numpy(wave).unsqueeze(0)          # [1, n_samples]

mel = torchaudio.transforms.MelSpectrogram(
    sample_rate=24000, n_fft=1024, win_length=1024,
    hop_length=256, n_mels=80, power=1.0,           # magnitude, not power
)(wave)

log_mel = torch.log(mel.clamp(min=1e-5))
print(log_mel.shape)      # [1, 80, n_frames],  n_frames = 1 + n_samples // 256

Three families

Tacotron-style models are autoregressive: each output is built from the outputs before it, one step at a time. It is the same loop as next-token prediction in Lesson 04, with mel frames in place of tokens. An attention mechanism looks at the phonemes and a decoder emits frames one by one, plus a "stop" signal to decide when to finish. Nothing forces that attention to move left to right, so it can skip a word or repeat one forever. And 500 frames means 500 sequential steps. Both facts made this family unsuitable for phones.

FastSpeech 2-style models are non-autoregressive. Each piece is named after the thing it does, which makes them the best family to learn first:

  • A duration predictor says how many mel frames each phoneme occupies.
  • A length regulator repeats each encoder vector that many times. It has no learned parameters at all.
  • A pitch predictor and an energy predictor add per-frame fundamental frequency and loudness into the hidden states.

The obvious question is what the duration predictor is trained against. The targets come from an alignment. For every training clip you need to know which frames belonged to which phoneme. FastSpeech 2 gets that from a separate forced aligner, run over the whole training set before training starts. A forced aligner is a small recogniser that already knows the words and only has to place their boundaries in time. That extra tool is the price of this design, and getting rid of it is exactly what VITS buys you below.

The loss is mean squared error on log(d + 1), where d is the number of frames. The logarithm is not decoration. Compare a 4-frame schwa with a 40-frame vowel. Being 4 frames wrong on the long one costs 16 in squared error. The same relative mistake on the short one is 0.4 frames, which costs 0.16 — a hundred times less. A raw squared error would therefore only ever care about the long sounds, and the short ones would drift. In log space, 4 → 5 costs 0.18 and 40 → 50 costs 0.22. Under raw squared error those same two mistakes cost 1 and 100.

length_regulator.pypython
import torch

h = torch.randn(1, 5, 192)                            # 5 phonemes: m ɒ d ə l
log_d = torch.tensor([[1.79, 2.71, 1.95, 1.61, 2.56]])  # what the predictor emits

d = (log_d.exp() - 1).round().clamp(min=1).long()     # tensor([[ 5, 14,  6,  4, 12]])

expanded = torch.repeat_interleave(h[0], d[0], dim=0).unsqueeze(0)
print(expanded.shape)                 # torch.Size([1, 41, 192])
print(d.sum().item() / 93.75)         # 0.437  -> seconds of audio

Two consequences follow, and both are the reason this family runs on phones. All 41 frames are produced in one forward pass, so inference is a matrix multiply rather than a loop. And duration, pitch and energy are ordinary tensors you can edit at inference time — you can slow the speech down or raise the pitch without retraining anything.

VITS is the third family, and the one behind Piper. It removes the mel spectrogram as an interface: text goes in and a waveform comes out of one model. Two things about it are worth your attention. There is no intermediate contract to get wrong, so the hop length bug above cannot happen. And monotonic alignment search learns the phoneme-to-frame alignment during training, using dynamic programming, so no forced aligner is needed. That is the payoff for the extra tool FastSpeech 2 demanded.

VITS also stacks two ideas this course does not teach: a conditional variational autoencoder and a normalizing flow. Both are ways of learning a flexible probability distribution over the audio. You do not need either one to use Piper, and the VITS paper is linked at the end of the lesson if you want the full story. The third idea, adversarial training, you do need, and the next section defines it. VITS reported a mean opinion score comparable to real recordings on the LJSpeech dataset, from a single-stage model.

Piper exposes VITS's sampling knobs directly. noise_scale (default 0.667) controls how much the timbre varies from one run to the next. Timbre is the colour of a voice: what makes two people saying the same word sound different. length_scale (default 1.0) is the speech rate, and bigger is slower. noise_w (default 0.8) is variation in the durations themselves.

12 · 5The vocoder: inventing phase

A mel spectrogram is not sound. The transform that produced it turned each window of audio into complex numbers, each with a magnitude and a phase. The spectrogram keeps the magnitudes and throws the phases away. Phase is where each wave sits in time — and without it there is no way to run the transform backwards.

So the last stage of every TTS system that goes through a spectrogram has to invent a phase which is consistent between overlapping frames. That stage is the vocoder.

Griffin-Lim is the naive baseline, and it is worth writing once by hand because it makes "inventing phase" concrete. Start from random phase. Build a complex spectrogram from the magnitude you were given and the phase you currently have. Convert to audio, convert back, and keep only the new phase. Repeat.

griffin_lim.pypython
import math, torch

n_fft, hop = 1024, 256
win = torch.hann_window(n_fft)

spec = torch.stft(wave, n_fft, hop, n_fft, win, return_complex=True)
mag  = spec.abs()                        # phase deleted here, on purpose

phase = torch.rand_like(mag) * 2 * math.pi
for _ in range(60):                      # 32 to 60 rounds is normal
    est  = torch.istft(mag * torch.exp(1j * phase), n_fft, hop, n_fft, win)
    back = torch.stft(est, n_fft, hop, n_fft, win, return_complex=True)
    phase = back.angle()                 # keep the new phase, discard its magnitude

out = torch.istft(mag * torch.exp(1j * phase), n_fft, hop, n_fft, win)

Listen to out. It is intelligible and it sounds metallic, like a robot from an old film. That metallic quality is the sound of inconsistent phase, and hearing it once teaches you more than any diagram.

Remember this — what "adversarial" means

Two networks are trained against each other. The generator produces audio. The discriminator has exactly one job: look at a clip and say whether it is a real recording or a generated one. The generator keeps improving until the discriminator can no longer tell. The pair is a generative adversarial network (GAN), and training them this way is called adversarial training.

Why not simply minimise squared error against the true waveform? Because two waveforms can sound identical and still disagree at every single sample, if one is shifted slightly in time. Squared error punishes that shift. So the safest thing the network can do is predict the average of all the plausible shifts — and the average of many shifted waves is close to silence. A discriminator never asks whether sample 4,001 matches. It only asks whether the result sounds real.

HiFi-GAN is the standard neural answer. Its generator is built from transposed convolutions: a convolution run backwards, so that instead of turning many samples into one number it turns one number into many samples. That is the machinery that takes 500 spectrogram frames up to 128,000 audio samples. It is trained against two discriminators. The important one is the multi-period discriminator. It reshapes the one-dimensional audio into two dimensions by a period taken from 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11, so it can see pitch periodicity that a plain distance loss never notices. HiFi-GAN reported 167.9 times real time on a V100 GPU, and a lightweight variant reached 13.4 times real time on a CPU.

Griffin-LimIterative phase estimation from magnitude onlyNo training; 32–60 transform rounds

The pattern in that table is worth stating as a rule: moving the upsampling out of transposed convolutions and into an inverse transform is the cheapest quality-preserving speed-up in on-device TTS. ISTFTNet and Vocos are both that idea.

Common trap

Do not quantize the vocoder first. In Lesson 07 you learned that weight-only INT8 is usually free; for a GAN generator that produces audio samples it is not. The errors arrive as an audible buzz, not as a slightly lower score. Quantize the acoustic model, keep the vocoder at FP16, and only then measure whether INT8 is acceptable by listening.

12 · 6Speech as a token stream

Everything so far needed a model per stage and a hand-designed spectrogram between them. None of that is shared with the language model you already know how to build. So people asked a different question: what if speech were just tokens?

The newer approach skips spectrograms entirely. Compress audio into discrete tokens with a neural codec, then train an ordinary decoder-only transformer to predict those tokens from text. TTS becomes next-token prediction, and every trick you learned in Lesson 04 applies unchanged.

The compression trick is residual vector quantization. An encoder produces one vector per frame. The first codebook picks its nearest stored entry; you subtract that entry; the second codebook quantizes what is left over; and so on. With 8 codebooks you get 8 tokens for every frame, ordered coarse to fine.

The maths, once
bitrate = frame rate × codebooks × log2(codebook size) Mimi: 12.5 × 8 × 11 = 1100 bits per second

Mimi runs at 12.5 frames per second with 8 codebooks of 2048 entries, which is 11 bits each. That is 1.1 kbps for speech you can still understand.

Careful with the word "frame" here. A codec frame is a much longer slice of time than a mel frame: 80 ms at 12.5 Hz, against 10.7 ms at 93.75 Hz. That is a factor of about seven and a half, and it is why so few tokens per second can still carry speech.

CodecSample rateFrame rateBitrateParameters

Two design details are worth teaching. Mimi splits meaning from timbre. Its first codebook is distilled to match a self-supervised speech representation. So the first token stream carries what was said, and the rest carry how it sounded. SNAC is hierarchical: coarse tokens run at a lower rate than fine ones, so one frame is not one token.

A transformer cannot predict a hierarchy. It predicts one flat stream. So the levels are interleaved, coarsest first, and Orpheus emits 7 SNAC tokens per coarse frame:

one frame, flattenedtokens
   c0   m0 m1   f0 f1 f2 f3  |  c0   m0 m1   f0 f1 f2 f3  |  c0 ...
   ^    ^^^^^   ^^^^^^^^^^^
   |    |       finest level, 4 per frame  (46.9 Hz)
   |    middle level, 2 per frame          (23.4 Hz)
   coarsest, 1 per frame (12 Hz) — carries most of the content

   1 + 2 + 4 = 7 tokens per frame. The model only ever sees this one line.

At 12 frames per second that is about 84 audio tokens for every second of speech.

codec_roundtrip.pypython
from snac import SNAC

model = SNAC.from_pretrained("hubertsiuzdak/snac_24khz").eval().cuda()

codes = model.encode(audio)        # a list of integer tensors, coarse to fine
audio_hat = model.decode(codes)    # and back to sound again

That round trip is the experiment to run. Encode a clip, decode it without changing anything, and listen. What you hear is the ceiling: no language model built on this codec can ever sound better than its own decoder.

This changes what "fast enough" means. A token-based synthesiser must generate roughly 84 to 100 tokens for every second of audio. It must also sustain that rate for as long as it speaks, because a token that arrives late is a gap in the sound. NeuTTS Air measured 20 tokens per second on a Galaxy A25 5G, against 111 tokens per second on an iMac M4.

Do the division yourself. You need 84 tokens per second; the phone produced 20; 84 / 20 is about 4.2. So that phone is roughly four times too slow to speak in real time on that backbone. The iMac, at 111 against 84, has 32 percent of headroom to spare. That gap is the whole story of on-device token TTS in 2026.

12 · 7Voices, cloning, and consent

There are three ways to tell a model whose voice to use, in increasing order of cost and capability.

A speaker embedding table. A learned matrix of shape [n_speakers, 256]; you add the right row to the encoder output. Cheap, and limited to voices that appeared in training. Multi-speaker Piper voices work this way.

A reference style vector. A fixed vector precomputed per voice, shipped as a small file beside the model. Kokoro ships 54 voices across 8 languages as separate vectors, and you can even blend two of them. Kokoro has no cloning: those vectors are the entire voice inventory.

In-context cloning. You prepend the audio tokens of a reference recording plus its transcript to the prompt, and the language model continues in that voice. NeuTTS Air clones from 3 to 15 seconds of clean mono audio. Voxtral TTS claims a custom voice from under 5 seconds.

This is a product decision, not only an engineering one

Three seconds of someone's voice is enough to copy it. If you ship cloning, you are shipping the ability to make a recording of a person saying something they never said. Decide three things before you write the code: whose recordings you accept and what consent you require, whether you watermark the output, and what you log. Kyutai's TTS deliberately ships no watermarking; other projects deliberately do. Either choice is defensible. Not having made the choice is not.

Prosody means rhythm, stress and intonation. In the FastSpeech 2 family it is explicit tensors you can edit. In token models it appears as inline tags in the text — Orpheus supports markers such as <laugh>. Neither approach is fully controllable in 2026. Prosody remains the largest quality gap between a small synthesiser and a human reading a long text aloud.

12 · 8Measuring it, and getting the first sound out fast

Speech quality has no loss function you can trust. The reference metric is the mean opinion score (MOS): human listeners rate clips from 1 to 5. Its absolute value is not comparable between papers, only within one listening test. The comparative mean opinion score (CMOS) is more reliable for small differences. There, listeners hear two clips side by side and score the difference from −3 to +3.

Because human tests are slow, people use predicted MOS instead. UTMOSv2 combines self-supervised speech features with a spectrogram-image model, and won first place in 7 of the 16 official metrics in the VoiceMOS Challenge 2024, Track 1. Use it as a regression test that tells you when a change made things worse. Never use it for a headline claim.

Intelligibility is easier and more useful. Run the speech recogniser from Lesson 11 over your synthesised audio and compare the transcript with the text you asked for. The word error rate (WER) catches skipped and repeated words, which is exactly the failure mode of attention-based synthesis. It also catches a bad normaliser, because "four point five zero dollars" is a transcript you can see.

Real-time factor and time to first audio

Two numbers, and they measure different things. People quote the first one and users feel the second one. The real-time factor (RTF) is compute time divided by audio length. The time to first audio (TTFA) is how long the person waits in silence.

RTF · real-time factor

  • compute time / audio duration. Lower is better.
  • RTF 0.3 means one second of speech costs 0.3 seconds of work.
  • Must stay below 1.0 or streaming has gaps.
  • Says nothing about waiting.

TTFA · time to first audio

  • Milliseconds from "text arrives" to "first sample can play".
  • This is what a person actually feels.
  • Target under 300 ms for a conversation. Over about 800 ms it feels like a bad phone line.
  • Can be terrible while RTF is excellent.
stream.pypython
import re, time

def phrases(text):
    return [s.strip() for s in re.split(r'(?<=[.!?])\s+', text) if s.strip()]

t0, first = time.perf_counter(), None
for chunk in phrases(paragraph):
    audio = synth(chunk)                       # your model, one phrase at a time
    if first is None:
        first = time.perf_counter() - t0       # time to first audio
    player.write(audio)                        # play while the next chunk is built

print(f"TTFA {first * 1000:.0f} ms")

Chunking costs you two things. Pitch resets at each boundary, so long text can sound like separate recordings glued together; you fix that by carrying decoder state or overlapping context across chunks. And if the instantaneous RTF ever rises above 1.0 in the middle of a sentence, the player runs out of audio and stutters. Keep 200 to 400 ms of audio buffered.

Getting audio in and out of the process

Every code sample so far assumed audio arrives in a variable and leaves in a player. On a real device neither is true. The microphone and the speaker are both driven by the sound card's clock, not by your loop, and they call you. If you block inside one of those calls, the hardware runs out of data and the user hears a click. So the pattern is always the same: two callbacks, two queues, and no model code between them.

duplex.pypython
import queue, sounddevice as sd

mic_q  = queue.Queue()      # microphone chunks, for the recogniser
play_q = queue.Queue()      # synthesised chunks of BLOCK samples, ready to play
BLOCK  = 512                # samples per callback: 32 ms at 16 kHz

def on_input(indata, frames, t, status):
    mic_q.put(indata.copy())              # copy: the driver reuses that buffer

def on_output(outdata, frames, t, status):
    try:
        outdata[:] = play_q.get_nowait()  # never block inside a callback
    except queue.Empty:
        outdata.fill(0)                   # underrun: play silence, not a click

def barge_in():
    """The user started talking. Stop speaking immediately."""
    while not play_q.empty():
        play_q.get_nowait()               # this flush is the whole feature

with sd.InputStream(samplerate=16000, blocksize=BLOCK, channels=1,
                    dtype='float32', callback=on_input), \
     sd.OutputStream(samplerate=24000, blocksize=BLOCK, channels=1,
                     dtype='float32', callback=on_output):
    run_assistant(mic_q, play_q, barge_in)   # your loop; the callbacks run above it

Three things in that file matter more than the rest. The input runs at 16 kHz and the output at 24 kHz, which is the resample from 12 · 1 made real. An empty output queue must produce silence rather than an exception, or one slow chunk becomes an audible fault. And barge_in is two lines of body: interrupting the assistant means emptying a queue, not stopping a model. Lesson 13 builds on all three.

The newest idea goes further and streams the input. Kyutai's Delayed Streams Modeling interleaves the text stream and the audio stream with a fixed delay. The synthesiser can therefore start speaking while a language model is still writing the rest of the sentence. Kyutai reports 220 ms from the first text token to the first audio chunk. Voxtral TTS reports 90 ms. Those are server numbers, but the shape of the idea is what Lesson 13 needs.

Size on disk is not latency

One vendor benchmark measured Piper at a 61 MB file but 2.6 GB of peak memory. The cause was the runtime's arena: a large block of memory reserved up front so that later allocations are fast. In the same test KittenTTS nano — the smallest file — had the worst time to first audio, because it synthesised in one shot. Treat that benchmark with care: it was published by a company selling a competing engine. The lesson stands anyway. In Lesson 08 you learned to measure rather than to reason about latency; measure resident memory and time to first audio on the real device.

Worth knowing

Almost every small voice in that table ships as ONNX, not as PyTorch. That is not an accident. A TTS pipeline is three or four separate graphs: front end, acoustic model, vocoder. The export path from Lesson 09 lets you run each one from Swift, from Kotlin or from a browser tab, without carrying Python along. The front end is the awkward part. eSpeak NG is a C library, so shipping it is a build problem and a licence problem, not an export problem.

Project 12

A voice that fits in 100 MB

Assemble a complete offline voice: a text front end you wrote, a model you chose on purpose, and a streaming loop. The whole package must fit in 100 MB. You must also be able to state its time to first audio and its word error rate from your own measurements, not from a model card.

  1. Install Kokoro and run the G2P snippet above. Print the phonemes for ten sentences from your own product, including a price, a date and an abbreviation.
  2. Write normalise(text): numbers, currency, dates, units and the ten abbreviations your product actually uses. Keep a test file of input and expected output pairs and run it on every change.
  3. Take an LJSpeech clip and resample it to 24 kHz first — LJSpeech is recorded at 22.05 kHz, and a sample-rate mismatch fails silently. Compute its mel spectrogram with the recipe above, then reconstruct it with the hand-written Griffin-Lim loop. Listen. This is what missing phase sounds like.
  4. Run the same sentences through Kokoro. Compare the two waveforms by ear, then run the Lesson 11 recogniser over both and compare word error rates.
  5. Measure one-shot TTFA on a paragraph of five sentences. Then add the chunking loop and measure again. Report both, plus RTF.
  6. Play the result through the duplex.py callbacks rather than a blocking call. Then call barge_in() mid-sentence and check that the voice stops within one block. Lesson 13 assumes you have this.
  7. Add up the bytes: model file, voice vectors, and the G2P data your front end needs. Stay under 100 MB, or write down exactly what you cut.

Then break it on purpose. Each of these fails in a different way, and the way it fails is the lesson:

  • Feed "$4.50 on 3/4/26" straight in, with no normaliser. Recognise the output. What is the word error rate now?
  • Write the 24 kHz audio to a file labelled 22050 Hz. Play it. The pitch and the speed both change, and nothing raised an error.
  • Chunk at every comma instead of every sentence. Listen for the prosody seams.
  • Add a 50 ms sleep inside the synthesis call so the instantaneous RTF crosses 1.0. Watch the buffer empty.

Check yourself

Your model has an RTF of 0.2 on the target phone. A user submits a 20-second paragraph and your app synthesises it in a single call. How long before they hear anything?

RTF 0.2 means 20 seconds of audio costs 4 seconds of compute, and a one-shot call plays nothing until every frame exists. RTF was fine the whole time; time to first audio was terrible. Chunking fixes it without touching the model.

A duration predictor emits [5, 14, 6, 4, 12] for five phonemes, and the frame rate is 93.75 frames per second. What happens next, and how long is the audio?

The length regulator is repeat_interleave and nothing else: 5 + 14 + 6 + 4 + 12 = 41 frames, and 41 / 93.75 = 0.437 s. Because the total length is known before any frame is computed, all 41 can be produced in one forward pass.

Your normaliser is correct. You printed the string and it says "four dollars fifty cents on March fourth twenty twenty six". But the voice still mispronounces your product's name, Ryzeon, as "rizz-ee-on". Where is the bug?

You saw the normalised string, so normalisation is not the suspect this time. A lexicon holds about 134,000 English words and your brand is not one of them, so the fallback guessed a pronunciation from the letters. Print the phonemes and you will see it at once. The fix is one lexicon entry, not more training.

You save a mel spectrogram to disk and later want the audio back. What is missing, and what are your two options?

The transform produced a magnitude and a phase for every bin, and the spectrogram kept only the magnitude. Without phase the transform cannot be run backwards. Griffin-Lim guesses by iterating, needs no training, and sounds metallic. A neural vocoder learns the mapping and sounds clean. Inventing that phase is the entire job of the last stage.

Key words from this lesson
TTS text to speech
The whole pipeline in this lesson: written text in, a playable waveform out.
Text normalisation often written TN
Rewriting written text into the words a voice must say, such as "$4.50" into "four dollars fifty cents".
Grapheme-to-phoneme often written G2P
The stage that turns letters into sound symbols, using a dictionary first and a fallback rule engine second.
Phoneme /ˈfoʊniːm/
A unit of sound that changes meaning. English has about 44 of them and only 26 letters.
Duration predictor
A small network that says how many spectrogram frames each phoneme should occupy.
Length regulator
The step that repeats each phoneme vector by its predicted duration. It has no learned parameters.
Forced aligner
A tool that places phoneme boundaries in a recording whose words are already known. It produces the duration predictor's training targets.
Adversarial training as in GAN
Training a generator against a discriminator whose only job is to tell real recordings from generated ones. It beats squared error, which averages waveforms into near-silence.
Phase
Where each wave sits in time. A spectrogram stores loudness but not phase, so something has to invent it.
Vocoder
The part that turns a spectrogram back into a sound wave, by inventing the missing phase.
Residual vector quantization often written RVQ
Compressing audio by quantizing a vector, then quantizing what the first codebook could not represent, and repeating.
WER word error rate
The share of words a recogniser gets wrong. Run it over your synthesised audio and it catches skipped words, repeated words and a bad normaliser.
RTF real-time factor
Compute time divided by audio length. Below 1.0 means the machine can keep up with speech.
Time to first audio also: TTFA
Milliseconds from receiving text to being able to play the first sample. The number a user actually feels.
