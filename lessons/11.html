Tiny speech recognition

A microphone gives you numbers. A transcript is letters. This lesson is the two bridges between them. First, turning sound into a picture a network can read. Then training a model when nobody told you which moment of sound is which letter.

11 · 1Sound is a list of numbers

The job of this lesson has a name: automatic speech recognition (ASR) — audio in, text out. It is also the ears of the offline voice assistant you will build in Lesson 13. Before any model can help, the sound has to become numbers, so start there.

A microphone measures air pressure many times per second. The result is a plain list of numbers, one per measurement. That is the whole raw format, and it has a name: PCM, pulse code modulation.

Two constants run through recognition in this course. The sample rate is 16,000 Hz, so one second of audio is 16,000 numbers. And each number is stored as a 16-bit signed integer, in the range −32768 to 32767. Models want float32 in the range −1 to 1, and the conversion is one division: x_float = x_int16 / 32768.0. Synthesis, in Lesson 12, runs at 24,000 Hz instead, so a resample between the two is a real pipeline step. Do not assume one rate everywhere.

Why 16 kHz? The Nyquist theorem says a sample rate of fs can represent frequencies up to fs / 2. At 16 kHz that ceiling is 8 kHz, and almost everything that separates one speech sound from another lives below it. Telephone audio samples at 8 kHz, so its ceiling is 4 kHz — which is exactly why /s/ and /f/ are hard to tell apart on a phone call. Above 16 kHz, extra samples stop helping and only cost compute.

Common trap

Forgetting the division by 32768. The numbers are then about 32,000 times too large — 90 decibels, if you prefer that unit. The features come out with values no trained model has ever seen, and the output is confident nonsense. Nothing raises an error. The same trap has a second form: feeding 44.1 kHz audio to a 16 kHz model. That does not error either. It merely makes every sound too low and too slow.

Raw samples are almost never fed to a model directly. A network would have to learn from scratch that sound is made of frequencies. So we do that part by hand, with maths we understand, before the first learned layer.

11 · 2Cut it up: frames, windows, the short-time Fourier transform

Speech is not stationary. The sound changes every few tens of milliseconds. So we cut the signal into short frames that are short enough to be almost unchanging inside, and take the frequency content of each one.

The numbers below are not a suggestion. They are what Whisper, NeMo, Kaldi and torchaudio all use, so an off-the-shelf model will expect exactly these.

ParameterValue at 16 kHzIn samples
Window length25 ms400  (n_fft)
Hop length (stride)10 ms160  (hop_length)
Overlap between frames15 ms240
Frame rate100 frames per second—

The window is 25 ms wide but moves only 10 ms, so consecutive frames overlap. Overlap means no information falls between two frames, and it makes the feature sequence change smoothly over time instead of jumping.

Each frame is multiplied by a window function — Hann in Whisper, Povey in Kaldi — before the transform. A raw rectangular cut has sharp edges, and sharp edges in time create fake energy at every frequency. That artefact is called spectral leakage. The window fades the frame to zero at both ends and removes most of it.

Then apply a fast Fourier transform (FFT) to each windowed frame. An FFT takes a slice of signal and reports how much energy sits at each frequency. Doing that to every frame in turn is the short-time Fourier transform (STFT).

Two numbers fall out of it, and both are worth deriving rather than memorising. The window holds 400 samples at 16,000 Hz, so each output bin covers 16000 / 400 = 40 Hz. And because the input is a real signal, the second half of the output mirrors the first. Only 400 / 2 + 1 = 201 bins carry new information.

Each of those 201 outputs carries a strength and a phase — where in its cycle that frequency was. Keep the strength, square it, throw the phase away. What is left is one column of 201 energies, once every 10 ms. A longer window gives finer frequency resolution but blurs time; a shorter one does the reverse.

11 · 3The mel scale, and how to read the picture

201 numbers per frame is more than we need, and they are spread over frequency in the wrong way. Human hearing is not linear in frequency. The difference between 100 Hz and 200 Hz is enormous. The difference between 4000 Hz and 4100 Hz is almost inaudible, even though both gaps are the same number of Hz.

The mel scale is a warped frequency axis built to match that perception. Here f is a frequency in Hz and m is the same frequency in mel:

m = 2595 · log10( 1 + f / 700 ) f in Hz, m in mel

Check it on three numbers. 1000 Hz gives 1000 mel, 2000 Hz gives 1521 mel, and 4000 Hz gives 2146 mel. Doubling the frequency does not double the mel value; the axis compresses as you go up. That is exactly why about 43 of the 80 filter centres end up below 2 kHz.

We place 80 filter centres at equal distance in mel. Mapped back to Hz they are narrow and crowded at the bottom and wide and sparse at the top. Each centre becomes a triangular filter, and the whole bank is one fixed matrix of shape (80, 201). Applying it is a single matrix multiply.

The last step is the log. Loudness perception is logarithmic, and a whisper and a shout differ by four or five orders of magnitude in energy. The log squeezes that range into one a network can train on. The result is the log-mel spectrogram, the input to nearly every speech model you will meet.

features.pypython
import torchaudio

# torchaudio.load already scales to float32 in [-1, 1].
# Raw microphone bytes do not: there you divide the int16 values by 32768.0.
wav, sr = torchaudio.load("command.wav")
if sr != 16000:
    wav = torchaudio.functional.resample(wav, sr, 16000)
wav = wav.mean(dim=0, keepdim=True)          # force mono -> (1, num_samples)

mel = torchaudio.transforms.MelSpectrogram(
    sample_rate=16000, n_fft=400, hop_length=160,
    n_mels=80, f_min=0.0, f_max=8000.0, power=2.0,
)
feats = (mel(wav) + 1e-10).log()             # (1, 80, T),  T = seconds * 100 + 1
                                             # the +1 is torchaudio's centre padding
print(feats.shape)

Two reasons this matters on a device. First, size: 30 seconds of float32 audio is 480,000 × 4 = 1.92 MB, while its log-mel is 80 × 3000 × 4 = 0.96 MB. Half the memory, and far fewer numbers for the first convolution to touch. Second, relevance: the 80 bands spend their resolution where speech information actually is.

Reading a spectrogram

A spectrogram has a strict meaning. Time runs across, one column per 10 ms frame. Mel band runs up, band 0 at the bottom near 0 Hz and band 79 at the top near 8 kHz. Brightness is log energy: bright means loud. Learn the four shapes below and you can debug an audio pipeline by looking at it.

What you seeWhat it isWhat it tells you
Evenly spaced horizontal stripesHarmonics of the voiceTheir spacing is the pitch: about 100 Hz apart for a typical male voice, about 200 Hz for a typical female voice
Bright bands that move and bendFormants — resonances of the mouthVowels are identified by where the first two formants sit; "ee" and "oo" look clearly different
A bright fuzzy block at the top, no stripesA fricative: s, sh, fNoise, not a tone. This is the energy an 8 kHz telephone throws away
A vertical gap, then a sudden vertical spikeA plosive: p, t, kSilence while the mouth is closed, then the burst when it opens

All four rows assume the usual colouring, where loud is bright. Plenty of tools ship an inverted colormap, where loud is dark, and then all four descriptions flip. Check which way your plot is coloured before you read anything into it.

Worth knowing

Whisper's feature extractor uses exactly n_fft=400, hop_length=160, feature_size=80 and a fixed chunk_length=30. The one real change across its versions is that whisper-large-v3 uses 128 mel bands instead of 80. If you swap a checkpoint and the model suddenly produces noise, check the number of mel bands before you check anything else.

11 · 4Nobody said which frame is which letter

Now the hard part. Five seconds of speech gives 500 feature frames. The transcript "turn on the kitchen light" is 25 characters. The digit classifier in Lesson 01 had a label for every image. A frame-wise classifier here would need a label for every frame. Nobody is going to mark where the k in "kitchen" begins, across thousands of hours of audio.

This is the alignment problem, and every architecture in this lesson is one answer to it. The first answer is connectionist temporal classification (CTC).

The encoder still produces one probability distribution per frame, but over vocab + 1 symbols. The extra symbol is the blank, written ε. Blank does not mean silence. It means "no new output at this frame". Then a fixed collapse rule B turns a frame sequence into text, in this order:

  1. Merge consecutive identical symbols.
  2. Delete every ε.
Remember this

The blank between the two ls in "hello" is load-bearing. Remove it and rule 1 merges the two ls into one, and the model can never write a double letter. That is the entire reason the blank symbol exists: it is a separator, not a silence detector.

The loss sums over every alignment

Many frame sequences collapse to the same text. CTC does not pick one of them and it never needs a frame-level label. It adds up the probability of all of them.

The maths, once

Name everything first. x is the audio, y is the correct transcript, and a is one path — one symbol chosen at every frame. B(a) is that path put through the collapse rule. And p_t(a_t) is the probability the model gave, at frame t, to the symbol the path chose there.

p( y | x ) = Σ over every path a with B(a) = y Π over frames t p_t( a_t ) loss = − log p( y | x )

Read it as: the probability of the correct text is the probability of any path that produces it. Training pushes that whole sum up, so the model discovers its own alignment. This is still the cross-entropy of Lesson 01 — the target is a set of paths rather than one class.

Do the sum once, by hand

That equation is easy to nod at and hard to believe. So here is the smallest version of it, small enough to check on a calculator. The vocabulary is a, b and the blank ε. The audio is three frames long, and the correct transcript is the single letter a. Suppose the model produced these per-frame probabilities — each row sums to 1, because each row is one softmax:

Framep(a)p(b)p(ε)

Which paths collapse to a? Any path with no b, at least one a, and all its as next to each other. There are six. Multiply each one out, one probability per frame:

So the loss is −log(0.510) = 0.673. Training nudges all six of those products up at once. No single path is ever named as the right one.

One path is deliberately missing from the table. a ε a has probability 0.7 × 0.6 × 0.1 = 0.042, but it collapses to aa, not a. Its two as are separated by a blank, so rule 1 cannot merge them. That single exclusion is the whole blank story again, now in arithmetic.

The forward algorithm, so the sum stays cheap

Six paths for three frames is fine. For 500 frames the count is astronomical, so nobody enumerates them. The forward algorithm does the same sum with dynamic programming, in time linear in the number of frames.

Name its four symbols before you read the equation. Write |y| for the number of letters in the transcript, so |y| = 5 for "hello". Next comes y′, read "y prime", the extended target. It is the transcript with a blank inserted before, between and after every letter. For "hello" that is ε h ε e ε l ε l ε o ε, which is 2|y| + 1 = 11 positions. And s is a position in y′, so here it runs from 1 to 11.

The fourth is α_t(s), said "alpha t of s". It is the total probability of every path that has consumed t frames and arrived at position s. That is the whole trick: one number holds a whole family of paths, so the family never has to be listed.

α_t(s) = p_t( y'_s ) · [ α_{t−1}(s) + α_{t−1}(s−1) + skip · α_{t−1}(s−2) ] skip = 1 only when y'_s ≠ ε and y'_s ≠ y'_{s−2}

Read the bracket aloud: to be at position s now, one frame ago you were at s, or at s − 1, or sometimes at s − 2. Then multiply by the model's probability at this frame for whatever symbol sits at s.

That skip condition is the collapse rule read backwards. You may not jump over a blank that sits between two identical labels, because that blank is the thing keeping them apart. The cost is T × (2|y| + 1) multiply-adds — linear in frames, not exponential. Run it on the three-frame toy above and it returns 0.510, the same number the table gave.

Decoding, and why CTC streams well

Greedy decoding takes the argmax at each frame and applies the collapse rule. It costs one pass and about ten lines of code.

Prefix beam search does better. A beam search keeps the k best partial answers alive instead of committing to one at every step, and scores each by its running log-probability. The prefix version keeps k candidate texts rather than paths, and merges every path that collapses to the same prefix. It typically removes 5 to 15 percent of the errors, and more with an external language model. That is a relative improvement, which §11·8 distinguishes carefully from an absolute one.

Why the gap? CTC assumes the outputs at different frames are conditionally independent given the encoder. There is no internal language model at all. That is what makes it cheap and perfectly parallel, and it is why CTC output can be phonetically plausible but linguistically wrong — "their" where "there" was meant.

The same assumption is CTC's gift to streaming. A frame's decision never depends on a later frame's decision. A causal encoder lets frame t look only at frames up to t, never at later ones. With one of those you can emit output the moment a frame arrives. And there is no autoregressive loop to unroll. Nothing here feeds its own last output back in as its next input. The language model of Lesson 04 does exactly that when it generates.

ctc.pypython
import torch.nn as nn

# encoder_out: (B, T, C) raw logits — batch, frames, vocab — blank at index 0
log_probs = encoder_out.log_softmax(dim=-1).permute(1, 0, 2)   # (T, B, C) is required
ctc = nn.CTCLoss(blank=0, reduction="mean", zero_infinity=True)
loss = ctc(log_probs, targets, input_lengths, target_lengths)

def collapse(seq, blank=0):          # greedy decode of one utterance
    out, prev = [], None
    for i in seq.tolist():
        if i != prev and i != blank:
            out.append(i)
        prev = i
    return out

ids = log_probs.argmax(-1).permute(1, 0)      # (B, T)
print(collapse(ids[0]))
Common trap

Two silent bugs live in that block. nn.CTCLoss wants (T, B, C) — frames first — not the (B, T, C) your model produces. PyTorch's own documentation calls the batch axis N; this course calls it B, and it is the same axis. It also wants log-probabilities, not logits — unlike nn.CrossEntropyLoss, which applies softmax itself. And the blank index is a convention: PyTorch defaults to blank=0, while many published vocabularies put blank last. Get it wrong and the loss still falls, slowly, toward a useless model.

11 · 5Transducers, and encoders that teach themselves

The conditional independence of CTC is its weakness. It has no idea what it just wrote, so it happily produces "their" where "there" was meant, and it cannot fit two letters into one frame. The transducer, usually called RNN-T (recurrent neural network transducer), fixes both by putting a small language model inside the recogniser. It has three networks:

  • Encoder — audio frames in, a vector h_t per frame out. The same job as the CTC encoder.
  • Prediction network — reads the previous non-blank output tokens and produces g_u. This is a small causal language model over the transcript.
  • Joint network — combines one h_t and one g_u, then a softmax over the vocabulary plus blank.

Decoding walks a grid. Frames run across it — T of them — and emitted tokens run down it, U of them. Call that grid the lattice. You start at the top-left corner and you must reach the bottom-right one. Two moves are allowed, and only two.

  • Emit the blank: step right, to the next audio frame, writing nothing.
  • Emit a token: step down, writing one token and staying on the same audio frame.

That second move is the whole point. One frame can step down twice, so it emits two tokens. CTC cannot do that at all, because CTC gets exactly one symbol per frame.

That is why transducers dominate on-device streaming. They are causal. They are also monotonic, meaning the path only moves forward through the audio, never back. So they never need the end of a sentence before committing to its start. The prediction network gives them an internal language model, so they beat CTC without an external one. And the state is small and cacheable, so the cost per chunk is constant no matter how long the user talks.

A recent variant, TDT (token-and-duration transducer), predicts the token and how many frames to skip. Skipping frames is the direct reason NVIDIA's Parakeet-TDT-0.6B-v3 reports an RTFx of 3332.74 — it does not look at most of the audio twice. RTFx is seconds of audio processed per second of compute, so higher is better; §11·7 defines it properly, together with the RTF it is the reciprocal of.

Self-supervised encoders

Where does a good encoder come from if your language has almost no transcripts? wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT answer that. Both learn from unlabelled audio, and then a small labelled set trains a CTC head on top.

wav2vec 2.0 works in four steps, and none of them needs a transcript. A convolution reads the raw waveform — no mel at all — and produces one vector every 20 ms. A Transformer mixes each vector with its neighbours. Each vector is then snapped to its nearest entry in a codebook: a fixed list of a few hundred prototype vectors. That turns it into a discrete label. Finally, training hides a short span and asks the model to pick its true label out of a set of wrong ones.

That last step is the learning signal, and it comes free from the audio itself. HuBERT does the same hiding, but its labels come from grouping earlier features into clusters with k-means, an algorithm that sorts vectors into k groups by similarity. The first round groups mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), an older and smaller cousin of the log-mel features of §11·3.

Here is the result that made the field pay attention. Pre-train on 53,000 hours of unlabelled audio, then fine-tune on 10 minutes of labelled speech. That takes wav2vec 2.0 LARGE to 4.8 % and 8.2 % WER on LibriSpeech test-clean and test-other. HuBERT LARGE reaches 4.7 % and 7.6 %. This is still the right starting point when your language has no released model.

11 · 6Whisper, and the 30-second box

CTC has no language model at all, and a transducer has only a small one. On hard audio both write things that sound right and read wrong. So ask the obvious question: what if the decoder were a full language model that had already read a great deal of text?

That is Whisper, and the price it pays is that it cannot stream. The third family treats speech recognition as ordinary sequence-to-sequence text generation. Whisper is a plain Transformer encoder-decoder: the encoder reads the spectrogram, the decoder writes tokens while attending to it, exactly the machinery of Lesson 03.

Its shapes are worth memorising, because they explain both its quality and its problem:

30 s at 16 kHz = 480,000 samples log-mel = (80, 3000) — 128 bands for large-v3 conv stem, stride 2 on time encoder states = 1500 — each one covers 20 ms

Whisper always processes exactly 30 seconds. Shorter audio is zero-padded to fill the box. A three-second command therefore costs the compute of a thirty-second meeting.

The decoder is steered entirely by control tokens in its prompt: <|startoftranscript|> <|en|> <|transcribe|> <|notimestamps|>. There is one token per supported language, 99 of them, and <|transcribe|> versus <|translate|> picks the task with the same weights. Drop <|notimestamps|> and it emits timestamps at 0.02 s resolution — exactly the 20 ms encoder stride.

Now the awkward part. Whisper cannot stream, for three independent reasons:

  1. The encoder uses full bidirectional self-attention over all 1500 positions. Position 10 attends to position 1499 — it needs the future.
  2. The input is a fixed 30-second block, so you must wait or pad.
  3. The decoder's cross-attention reads the entire encoder output.

Chunked "streaming Whisper" wrappers exist. They re-run the encoder for every chunk and duplicate or truncate words at the seams. Treat them as an approximation, not as streaming.

11 · 7Streaming for real: chunk, look-ahead, delay

A streaming encoder replaces unlimited attention with a bounded context. There are three knobs, and together they are the entire latency story.

  • Causal (left-only) attention: frame t attends to [t − left, t], where left is how many past frames it may see. Zero added delay, worst accuracy.
  • Chunked attention: process a block of chunk frames together; inside the block frames see each other freely. The delay is the chunk duration.
  • Look-ahead: allow right frames of future context beyond the chunk. These are encoder frames, not the 10 ms feature frames of §11·2. A streaming encoder subsamples time by 8, so one encoder frame covers 80 ms. Each look-ahead frame buys accuracy and costs that 80 ms.

NVIDIA writes this as att_context_size = [left, right], counted in encoder frames, and publishes the result for the same weights at four settings. This one table is the whole trade-off in real numbers:

Chunkatt_context_sizeAverage WER, 8 test sets
80 ms[70, 0]8.43 %
160 ms[70, 1]not published
560 ms[70, 6]not published
1.12 s[70, 13]6.93 %

The model card publishes only the two extremes, so the two middle rows are honestly blank. It does say which way the curve bends, and the shape is the usual one. Most of the gain arrives in the first few hundred milliseconds. The last doubling of delay buys very little.

Fourteen times more delay buys 1.5 points of absolute WER — 8.43 down to 6.93. Same weights, one runtime flag. That is the decision you will actually make when you ship: a dictation app can afford 1.12 s, a voice assistant that must feel alive cannot.

"Cache-aware" means the encoder keeps its key/value cache between chunks. That is the same idea as the KV cache from Lesson 04. Each new chunk then costs only its new frames. The older approach re-ran the encoder over an overlapping window and wasted up to three times the compute.

latency ≈ chunk duration + look-ahead + compute time per chunk

You keep up with real time only if the compute term is smaller than the chunk duration. That ratio is the RTF, the real-time factor: processing_time / audio_duration, and it must be below 1. The Open ASR Leaderboard reports RTFx, which is 1 / RTF, so there higher is better. They are reciprocals, and confusing them is a classic way to report a result backwards.

Do not run the big model on silence

Most of the audio from a microphone is not speech. Running an ASR model on it wastes battery, and Whisper in particular hallucinates on empty audio — "Thank you for watching!" on a silent recording is a well-known failure. A voice activity detector (VAD) gates the expensive model: a tiny network that answers only "is anyone speaking right now?".

Silero VAD is the usual choice — about 2 MB on disk, small enough that you put it in front of everything. Lesson 13 takes it apart properly. Deciding when a turn actually ended is much harder than deciding whether the current 32 ms holds speech. That decision is what makes an assistant feel rude or patient. Here you only need the call:

gate.pypython
from silero_vad import load_silero_vad, read_audio, get_speech_timestamps

model = load_silero_vad()
wav = read_audio("meeting.wav")            # resamples to 16 kHz for you
segments = get_speech_timestamps(wav, model, return_seconds=True)
print(segments)                            # [{'start': 1.28, 'end': 4.96}, ...]

A production on-device pipeline is then: microphone → ring buffer → resample to 16 kHz → VAD on 32 ms chunks. If that says speech, the streaming encoder runs on 320 ms chunks with 80 ms look-ahead, and the transducer decodes. Partial text appears, and you are allowed to rewrite it. Then the turn ends and you finalise the segment. That last step is the interesting one, and it is the subject of Lesson 13.

11 · 8Measuring it, and adapting it

A model that is not measured is a model you cannot improve, and speech has one measure everyone quotes. Word error rate (WER) is word-level edit distance divided by the length of the reference. Compute it once by hand and you will never misread a leaderboard again.

The maths, once
WER = ( sub + del + ins ) / n_ref_words sub words replaced by a wrong word del words the model failed to write ins words the model wrote that nobody said n_ref_words how many words the reference actually contains

The denominator is the reference length only. Insertions are not bounded by it, so WER can be larger than 100 % when a model produces more wrong words than there were true words.

The same computation over characters is the character error rate (CER). For the reference "boston" and the hypothesis "bostin", CER is 1 / 6 = 16.7 %. CER is the right metric for languages written without spaces between words, and it is a fairer view when a model gets a word nearly right.

Remember this

Papers report improvements two ways, and the two look very different. Absolute means points of WER: 8.4 % down to 6.9 % is 1.5 points absolute. Relative means the fraction of the errors removed: the same change is 1.5 / 8.4 = 18 % relative. Relative numbers are always the bigger ones, which is why marketing prefers them. Always check which one you are reading before you compare two results.

score.pypython
from jiwer import wer, cer

ref = "i would like to book a flight to boston"
hyp = "i would like book a flight two boston today"

print(wer(ref, hyp))              # 0.3333...  -> 33.3 %
print(cer("boston", "bostin"))    # 0.1666...  -> 16.7 %
Common trap

Text normalisation moves WER more than most model changes do. Casing, punctuation, "1st" against "first", "%" against "percent" — a raw comparison can report roughly double the WER of a normalised one. Whisper ships an EnglishTextNormalizer for this reason. Always say which normaliser produced a number. And remember what the test set is. LibriSpeech test-clean is read audiobooks in quiet rooms. Real WER on noisy far-field speech is often three to five times higher.

Fine-tuning for a new accent or language

Two recipes, chosen by how much labelled audio you have.

CTC head on a self-supervised encoder

  • Best with 1 to 50 hours, and for a language the base model never saw.
  • Build a character or BPE (byte-pair encoding, Lesson 03) vocabulary from your transcripts; reserve one index for blank.
  • Freeze the convolutional feature extractor with model.freeze_feature_encoder().
  • Wav2Vec2ForCTC, ctc_loss_reduction="mean", learning rate around 1e-4 with warmup, and SpecAugment on. SpecAugment blanks out a few mel bands and a few time spans of each spectrogram during training. The model then cannot lean on any one of them.

Whisper sequence-to-sequence fine-tune

  • Best for accent and domain adaptation, and languages Whisper already partly knows.
  • LoRA (low-rank adaptation) on the attention projections fits a whisper-small run in about 8 GB and finishes in hours, not days.
  • Freeze the encoder below roughly 10 hours of data, or the model overfits and loses its robustness.
  • Merge the adapter before exporting to ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange). The other route is the GGML format whisper.cpp expects. GGML is the older tensor library and file format behind whisper.cpp and llama.cpp. Its successor is the GPT-Generated Unified Format (GGUF) of Lesson 07, and the two are not interchangeable.

Accents need surprisingly little data. Five to ten hours of the target accent usually removes a large fraction of the remaining errors. The encoder is already strong, and you are mostly re-weighting the decoder's expectations. A new language with a different script is a different project: vocabulary work plus 100 hours or more, or the self-supervised route above.

finetune.pypython
from transformers import WhisperForConditionalGeneration, WhisperProcessor

processor = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-small",
                                             language="hindi", task="transcribe")
model = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-small")

# forced_decoder_ids on the model config is deprecated. Set the task here instead.
model.generation_config.language = "hindi"

Two lessons hide in that table. First, the decoder decides speed far more than size does. Parakeet CTC 1.1B runs at RTFx 2793.75, against Whisper large-v3 at RTFx 68.56. That is a 40× gap for about a quarter point of WER. Second, quantization from Lesson 07 pays here. A 2026 paper took Nemotron Speech Streaming from 2.47 GB to 0.67 GB with int4 k-quantization. Average WER came out at 8.20 %, against the 8.43 % that §11·7 gives for the 80 ms streaming configuration. It then ran faster than real time on CPU, with no GPU, at 0.56 s of latency.

The Flavors of Moonshine work adds a rule for everything in Part V: at 27M parameters, monolingual models beat multilingual ones. Their per-language tiny models remove 48 % of Whisper Tiny's errors — a relative figure — and match Whisper Medium, which is 28 times larger. At small scale, pick one language. Project 11

Offline speech-to-text on a phone

Build the whole path once. Raw audio to features you made yourself, then a small model you trained yourself. Then a real recogniser, measured honestly and packed small enough to run with no network. Rung A trains in minutes and proves your front end is correct. Rung B is the real thing.

  1. Look at the sound. Load one clip, resample to 16 kHz, build the log-mel with features.py above, and plot it. Find a vowel, a fricative and a plosive with your eyes before you train anything.
  2. Rung A — keyword spotting. Take Speech Commands v0.02 from the parquet mirror pollen-robotics/speech-commands-v0.02: 35 words, 1-second clips at 16 kHz. Features [B, 1, 64, 101], then the same kind of convolutional network (CNN) you built in Lesson 02, about 200k parameters, cross-entropy, AdamW. Ten epochs should reach about 95 % accuracy.
    Two numbers there deserve a note, because they are not the 80 and 100 of §11·3. We drop to 64 mel bands on purpose: one-second keyword clips do not need the top detail, and the CNN gets smaller. And it is 101 frames, not 100, because torchaudio pads half a window onto each end by default, so a T-second clip gives T · 100 + 1 frames. An off-by-one here costs an evening.
  3. Rung B — a real recogniser. Fine-tune openai/whisper-tiny.en (39M parameters, Apache-2.0) on openslr/librispeech_asr, split train.clean.100 — 100 hours, about 28k utterances, parquet, so it loads with datasets 5. One thousand steps is enough to see the WER move.
  4. Score it. Run score.py on the held-out set twice: once on raw text, once through Whisper's EnglishTextNormalizer. Write both numbers down. The difference is the point.
  5. Put it on the device. Two honest routes. For batch transcription, build whisper.cpp v1.9.2 and run an int8 model with its built-in VAD. For real streaming, run a streaming Zipformer transducer through sherpa-onnx v1.13.5, which has Swift and Kotlin bindings and ships an int8 encoder of about 68 MB.
  6. Measure it. Time 20 utterances with a warm-up and take the median, as in Lesson 08. Report RTF, not "it felt fast". Then compare the RTF of the padded 30-second path with the streaming one. This number is the "ears" line of the latency budget you will assemble in Lesson 13.

Then break it on purpose. Each of these fails silently in production:

  • Feed the model 44.1 kHz audio without resampling. Read the transcript. Nothing errors.
  • Remove the division by 32768 from a raw PCM path and look at the feature values.
  • Pass (B, T, C) to nn.CTCLoss, which wants (T, B, C), or move the blank index to the last position. Watch the loss fall toward a useless model.
  • Run Whisper on 30 seconds of silence with the VAD switched off, and count the invented sentences.

Check yourself

A 2.5-second clip at 16 kHz goes through n_fft=400, hop_length=160, n_mels=80, with torchaudio's default centre padding. What is the shape of the log-mel tensor?

The first axis is the number of mel bands, so 80 — the 201 FFT bins were already collapsed by the filterbank. The second is frames: 2.5 s × 16000 = 40,000 samples, divided by the 160-sample hop, gives 250, and centre padding adds the one extra frame.

A CTC model emits this frame sequence, where ε is the blank: ε t t ε o o ε o. What text does the collapse rule produce?

Merge identical neighbours first: ε t ε o ε o. Then delete the blanks: "too". The order matters. Delete the blanks first and the two runs of o touch, merge into one, and you get "to" — which is exactly the bug the blank exists to prevent.

A reference has 6 words. The model outputs 8 words: 5 correct, 1 wrong word in place of a correct one, and 2 words that were never said. What is the WER?

One substitution plus two insertions plus zero deletions is 3 errors, divided by the 6 reference words: 3 / 6 = 50 %. The denominator is always the reference length, never the hypothesis length.

You need partial text on screen within about 200 ms of the user speaking, on a phone, offline. Which architecture fits?

Whisper's encoder is bidirectional over a fixed 30-second block, so it structurally cannot answer in 200 ms. A transducer is causal and monotonic, keeps its cache between chunks, and its delay is the chunk plus the look-ahead you choose.

Key words from this lesson
Sample rate 16,000 Hz for speech
How many times per second the microphone measured air pressure. One second of speech audio is 16,000 numbers.
Frame and hop 25 ms and 10 ms
The short slice of audio we analyse, and how far we move before taking the next slice. Slices overlap on purpose.
Mel scale /mel/
A warped frequency axis on which equal distances sound equally different to a human ear.
Log-mel spectrogram
The picture of sound that speech models read: 80 frequency bands, about 100 columns per second at 16 kHz with a 10 ms hop, values compressed by a logarithm.
Blank written ε
An extra output symbol meaning "no new letter at this frame". It is a separator, not silence.
Collapse rule
Merge neighbouring identical symbols, then delete every blank. This turns a frame sequence into text.
Transducer also: RNN-T, recurrent neural network transducer
A recogniser with three parts — encoder, prediction network, joint network — that can emit several tokens from one audio frame.
Monotonic
A decoder that only ever moves forward through the audio, never back. This is what lets a transducer commit to early words before the sentence has finished.
Look-ahead
How many future frames a streaming model is allowed to see. More accuracy, more delay.
RTF and RTFx
Processing time divided by audio duration, and its reciprocal. RTF must be under 1; RTFx must be over 1.
WER word error rate
Substitutions plus deletions plus insertions, divided by the number of words that were actually said.
CER character error rate
The same edit-distance count over characters instead of words. The right metric for scripts written without spaces, and fairer when a word is nearly right.
Absolute and relative
Two ways to report a gain. 8.4 % down to 6.9 % is 1.5 points absolute, and 18 % relative. Relative always sounds larger.
